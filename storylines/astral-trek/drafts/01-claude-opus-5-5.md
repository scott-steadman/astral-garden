# Astral Trek, Episode 1: "The Choir Beyond the Barrier"

## I. The Captain's Log

The pen was too small for his hand. It always was.

Captain Galen Underbough held it the way he held a sparrow, two fingers and a thumb and a great deal of care, and bent his head over the logbook resting on his knee. He was a firbolg, nine feet of grey-blue hide and moss-coloured beard folded into a captain's chair that the shipwrights at Rosste had rebuilt twice to fit him. The bridge smelled of cold stone and lamp oil, and faintly, always, of the sea. That was Ship. A sea-elf's soul left salt in the air the way a baker left flour.

Somewhere in the walls, a harp was playing something light and wandering. A rat was running for its life across the far end of the bridge. A faerie dragon the colour of a soap bubble was gaining on it.

Galen wrote:

> **Captain's log, stardate 1.712.**
>
> *Fifth week out from Rosste. All hands well.*
>
> *Two days ago we skirted a becalming at 371.-139.58, a dead zone roughly four leagues across, which Ship has named "the Glass" because nothing in it moves and the light lies flat on it like a pane. We charted it as a hazard and gave it a wide berth. I note it here for whoever comes after us: a ship that loses its will in there will sit until it drifts out, and nobody knows how long that is.*
>
> *Today we reach an uncharted planar system at 388.-142.61, drift 1.0.-2, measured this day. Ship's long-scrying shows two planes in close proximity: one apparently whole, one apparently broken. We will approach with caution and attempt first contact if there is anyone to contact.*
>
> *The crew is in good spirits. Ship continues to play during my log entries. I have asked him not to. He continues.*
>
> *G. Underbough, Captain, A.F.S.* Tranelis Selevarun

He signed it with a flourish he'd practised as a boy for a career in letters he never had. He blew on the ink, closed the book, and lifted the panel in the arm of his chair. The compartment beneath was lined with sea-green felt. He laid the book in it and shut the lid.

"Oh Captain, my Captain," said the bridge, in a warm tenor that came from nowhere in particular. "That was a lovely entry. The bit about the Glass was almost poetic."

"The bit about the music was a formal complaint."

"I took it as a review."

---

Four hundred leagues away and instantly, in the Hall of Records at Fleet Headquarters in Rosste, a logbook on a lectern began to write itself.

Senior Crewmate Tibbet Quarry, crewgnome, archivist, and on this particular evening the only living soul in the Hall, put down his tea and watched. He never tired of it. The ink bloomed up out of the vellum a word at a time, dark blue, in a large careful hand that pressed hard on its downstrokes. *Captain's log, stardate 1.712.* Thirty-one lecterns stood in the Hall, one for every ship in the Fleet, each with its book; most were quiet at this hour. This one hummed faintly, as the permanent ones did, the hum of a ninth-level sending that never closed.

The ink reached *He continues.* and Tibbet laughed out loud, alone among the lecterns. He made a note in the index: *Tranelis Selevarun, 1.712: new hazard (becalming, "the Glass"); new system 388.-142.61.* Then, because it was his habit, he read the signature twice, the way you'd check a friend's face for tiredness.

The hand was steady. Good.

## II. The Broken Moon

The rat lost.

Kono landed on the arm of the captain's chair with the creature limp in her jaws and an expression of enormous self-satisfaction. She was the size of a house cat, iridescent from snout to tail, and her wings were thin as the skin on warm milk. She did not need to flap them to hover. She flapped them anyway, because people looked.

"Fourteen this week, Big Moss," she said, through rat. "I'm keeping a tally."

"On what?"

"On Sprocket's workbench. In chalk. He hasn't noticed."

"He'll notice," said Galen. "Take it below. Not on the stone."

She took it below, which meant she dropped it down the nearest grate with a sound like a wet glove. The harp in the walls did a little glissando of applause.

Galen breathed in. He smelled what he always smelled on this bridge: Ship, salt and something like sunlit kelp, chaotic good, a little reckless, entirely kind. Kono, lemon peel and black pepper, chaotic good with a mean streak for rodents. And, coming up the stairs, warm bread and ink: Commander Reva Castellane, lawful good, first officer, carrying two mugs.

The gift had come with his oath. He had been a boy in the Dimmerwood, kneeling in front of an oak older than his grandmother's grandmother, swearing to keep the green things of the world alive, and when he stood up every living person around him had a smell that wasn't their body. It had taken him years to learn the vocabulary. Good was bread, or rain, or cut grass. Law was something clean and mineral: chalk, slate, ink. Chaos was citrus and spice. Evil was burnt hair. He had smelled that only a handful of times in his life, and every time he'd remembered it for a year.

"You'll want this," said Reva, handing up a mug. She was human, fifty, with a long jaw and a merchant captain's habit of counting things. "We're three minutes out. Ship's being coy."

"I am not being coy," said Ship. "I am building suspense. Are you both looking at the scryer? Look at the scryer."

They looked.

The scryer was a disc of black water set into the floor at the centre of the bridge, ringed by empty stations: navigation, arcana, tinkering, security, all of them dark, because Ship ran nearly everything himself. Light swam up out of the water and stood in the air over it.

The astral sea was silver-violet and endless. In it hung two planes.

The nearer one was whole, a blue-green world in a skin of faintly shimmering barrier, like a bubble of oil on water. The farther one was a ruin. It had been a sphere once, a pale and beautiful one, white and gold, and now it was a sphere with a bite out of it. Debris trailed away from the wound in a long glittering comet's tail. Chunks of white stone the size of mountains turned slowly in the silver.

"A god's home plane," said Reva quietly. "And no barrier."

"A dead god's," said Galen.

Nobody said anything for a moment. You read about such things. You did not expect to see one in your fifth week.

"I've sent for Hadrin," said Ship. "He's going to be insufferable. Also, there's this."

The image swung back to the living world and enlarged. Galen leaned forward. Around the blue-green bubble, just outside its barrier, hung a haze. It was faint, like breath on cold glass, and it glittered.

"Dust?" said Reva.

"No," said Ship. "It's moving against the drift. Dust doesn't do that. Fish do that."

"Fish."

"I don't know what it is, Commander. I'm saying it moves like a shoal."

The harp in the walls had gone on playing the whole time, a soft looping figure. Now, in the middle of a bar, it stopped.

The silence was so sudden that Kono, coming back up the stairs, stopped on the top step with one paw raised.

"Ship?" said Galen.

"I can hear singing," said Ship. His voice was very quiet. "From the haze. Lots of voices. I think—" He stopped. "Captain, I think it's a choir."

## III. Briefing

The briefing room smelled of beeswax and the cedar of the long table, and of the people around it.

Lieutenant Commander Hadrin Morrowmere, Chief Arcanist, smelled of cold iron and old paper: lawful neutral, and proud of it. He was a dwarf with a white beard plaited into two thick cables and spectacles he did not need, which he wore because divination spells made his eyes ache and the lenses gave him something to take off when he was annoyed. He was annoyed now. He had been woken.

Lieutenant Pell Fennimore, Chief Tinkerer, smelled of machine oil, burnt toast, and something cheerful and green: neutral good. He was a gnome with a burn scar across the back of one hand and a pair of magnifying goggles pushed up into hair that had been brown before the workshop accident of last spring and was now a permanent, slightly singed orange. There was chalk on his sleeve. He had noticed the tally.

Lieutenant Zahra Emberly, Security Chief, smelled of whetstone and woodsmoke: lawful good, a sharp clean version of it. She was a tiefling, red as brick, with horns that swept back and down like a ram's and a glaive she had leaned against the wall within reach of her chair. She did not sit in rooms with her back to doors.

Reva and Galen made six with Kono, who was on the table, which she was not allowed to be.

"Pokey," Kono said to Zahra, by way of greeting.

"Don't."

"Pokey."

"Ship," said Galen. "Play it for them."

Ship played it.

It came out of the cedar walls, out of the beams, out of the air: voices. Hundreds of them. Thin and high, low and grainy, children and old men and women, singing a slow tune in a minor key, something that rose and fell like a tide coming in. It was in a language none of them knew, and it was beautiful, and the hair on the back of Galen's neck stood up.

When it stopped, Pell had taken his goggles off and was holding them in both hands.

"What are the words?" said Reva.

"I don't know yet. The earpieces will tell us if we go close enough to talk. But the tune—" Ship's voice did the thing it did when he was thinking hard, losing its showman's polish. "Commander, I'm a bard. I've been a bard for longer than I've been a ship. And I've never heard a choir that size keep that well in time. It's not human. It's not *people*. Hundreds of people don't breathe together like that unless someone's conducting them very, very hard."

"Could be a ritual," said Zahra. "Drilled for years."

"Could be."

Hadrin had his spectacles off and was polishing them on his beard. "I want to talk about the other plane first," he said. "Because it is much more important and nobody is listening to me."

"I'm listening," said Galen.

"You are being polite, which is different." He put the spectacles back on. "That is a god's home plane, and its barrier is gone. The Fleet's survey texts are very clear: there is only one way that happens. A god whose home plane is destroyed is homeless but alive. A god killed *away* from home is banished home and reforms. But a god killed *on* its home plane is destroyed for good, and its barrier goes with it. The plane begins to decay. And decay"—he raised one thick finger—"is a clock."

"How long?" said Reva.

"Give me a day with the debris and I'll give you a number. Give me an hour and I'll give you a guess. My guess is three centuries, give or take a few decades. Those fragments have travelled a long way down that tail."

"So the god on the living plane—"

"Is dead. Has been dead for three hundred years. Whoever lives down there lost their god before their great-great-great-grandparents were born." Hadrin sat back. "Which means no divine magic, unless someone's been very clever with holy symbols. And it means the souls of their dead have had nowhere to go."

There was a pause.

"Say that again," said Galen.

"A whole soul, when its body dies, chooses. Die for good. Go back to its god and merge. Or languish on its god's home plane until the god needs it." Hadrin spread his hands. "No god to merge with. No home plane to languish on. If they don't choose to end, they drift. Out in the astral sea, lost, with nothing between them and—well. Anything."

Ship said, very softly, "Oh."

Everyone looked at the walls, as if they could see the haze through them.

"It's them," said Pell. "Isn't it. The shoal. It's three hundred years of their dead."

"And they're singing," said Reva.

"To whom?" said Zahra.

Nobody answered.

Galen put both enormous hands flat on the table. "Here's what we do. Pell, I want a drone down to the plane: totem components, so we can shift people later if we need to. And recordings. If the dead are singing, I want to know whether the living can hear them. Hadrin, give me your number, the real one, as fast as you can. Zahra, I want the ship at stations while we're near that haze. Reva—"

"You want me to start a list of things that are about to go wrong."

"I want you to start a list of things that are about to go wrong."

"Already started," said Reva, and tapped her temple.

## IV. What the Drone Heard

Ship opened a portal for the drone, because shifting to a plane needs a totem and you can't make a totem until you've been there.

He opened it small, as a courtesy. It was still a production. A point of light appeared in the air of the drone bay and hissed like a kettle; the hiss rose in pitch as the point stretched into a bright line, the line split and spread into a glowing rectangle, and the rectangle swelled and shaped itself until it stood eight feet tall: a golden door, carved all over with waves and leaping fish, with a brass ring for a knocker. Even at drone size Ship couldn't help himself. It swung open on a smell of salt marsh and rain.

"You did the fish," said Pell.

"I always do the fish."

The drone, a brass-and-glass thing like a dragonfly the size of a dog, buzzed through. The door swung shut and folded down into a line and a point and was gone.

It came back forty minutes later through a second, smaller door, with its collecting baskets full: a handful of grey salt, three dried reeds, a piece of driftwood worn silver, and a curl of greenish bronze that looked like it had broken off a bell. Pell laid them out on his workbench, which said *KONO 14* in chalk along its front edge, and began the long work of making a totem.

Then he played back what the drone had heard.

He did it in the workshop, with just Galen and Hadrin, because Galen had a feeling. The workshop smelled of solder and Pell's toast. The drone's memory came out of a little horn like a gramophone's.

Wind. Reeds hissing. The slap of water on wood. A town: shutters, a dog, a cart's wheel on stones. And over it, from the sky, faint, the choir.

Then a woman's voice, close, the drone hovering near a window. The earpiece in Galen's ear turned her words to Common as she spoke. She sounded tired, and she sounded like she was talking to someone she loved.

"I know, sweetheart. I know. I hear you. Every night, I hear you." A pause. "Tomorrow. He says tomorrow, at the high tide. He says if I ring it the door will open and you'll all come home." A longer pause. A sound that might have been a laugh or not. "I'm frightened, Lira. Isn't that silly? I'm frightened of the one thing I've wanted for two years."

The recording ended with the drone moving on.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"'He,'" said Hadrin at last.

"'If I ring it, the door will open,'" said Galen.

"I don't like either of those," said Pell. He had gone pale under the soot. "Captain, I don't like either of those at all."

---

## V. The Voice in the Haze

The thing came to them that evening, ship's time, while they were still fifty leagues off the haze.

Ship saw it first. He dropped the harp mid-note again, and Galen, who was in his ready room reading Hadrin's preliminary figures, was on the bridge before the silence was a minute old. Reva was already there. Zahra came up the stairs at a run with the glaive.

In the scryer, between them and the haze, something hung in the silver.

It looked, at first, like a man. A very tall man, robed in white and gold, with a face so calm and kind it hurt to look at. Then Ship enlarged the image and it looked like a man drawn by someone who had heard men described. The robe moved like weed in water. The hands had too many joints. And around it, like a train, drifted a little cloud of the glittering haze, and from the cloud came the singing.

"*Travellers,*" it said. The voice came through the scryer and through the stone, warm and enormous. "*Welcome. You have come a long way through the dark. You are the first in three hundred years. I am Ammaduin, who made this world. I have come home to my children.*"

Galen breathed in through his nose.

There it was. Underneath the salt of Ship and the bread of Reva and the whetstone of Zahra, coming up out of the scryer like smoke from under a door: burnt hair. Wet ash. And on top of it, thick and sweet and wrong, the smell of fruit going soft in a bowl. Chaotic evil, wearing a perfume.

He'd never smelled the perfume before. He thought he knew what it meant.

"Ship," he said, very evenly. "Don't answer it."

"I wasn't going to."

"*You are wary,*" said the thing. "*That is wise. The sea is full of liars.*" It smiled. "*I am not one. I ask nothing of you. In a day my children below will open the way, and the dead will go home to the living, and I will go home to my world. It will be a beautiful thing to see. You may watch, if you like.*"

It turned and drifted back toward the haze, unhurried, trailing its choir.

When it was gone, Reva said, "Well. I think we've found 'he.'"

"It's lying," said Galen. "It's a fiend. Chaotic evil. I could smell it through the water."

"I believe you," said Reva. "Can you prove it?"

Galen opened his mouth and shut it again.

"Captain, I've known you three months and I would follow your nose into a burning building. But the woman in that recording isn't going to. Nor are the hundreds of dead people singing for it. You're going to walk up to a grieving mother and say, 'Excuse me, your god smells bad.'"

"It does smell bad."

"I know it does."

Galen sat down in the captain's chair. It creaked. He looked at the place where the thing had been.

"Then we'd better find something that doesn't need my nose," he said.

---

## VI. The Seal

Ship had a theory, and it was a bard's theory, so he wanted to sing it.

"Hear me out," he said. They were in the briefing room again: Galen, Reva, Hadrin, Pell. Zahra had the bridge. Kono was in the rafters, eating something. "A choir that size doesn't keep time like that unless something is driving it. So I did what you do when a choir sounds wrong. I took it apart."

"Took it apart," said Hadrin.

"Voice by voice. It took me most of the afternoon. I'm a ship, I've got a great deal of brain and not much to do with it on long voyages." The cedar walls hummed. "Listen. This is one woman."

A single voice came into the room, an old woman singing the tide-tune, thin and cracked.

"And this is a boy."

A child's voice, clear, a little flat.

"And this is a man who was very bad at singing in life and is still very bad at it."

Pell laughed despite himself.

"There are four hundred and eleven of them, that I can separate. Now. Hadrin. You know the thing about true names."

Hadrin's eyebrows went up. "I know several things about true names."

"Every soul has one. And a whole soul's name ends in a closing sound: the seal. A sundered soul's name has no seal. It's cut off. Anyone who's studied names can hear the difference."

"Yes. In a name."

"In a *name*. But a name is a sound a soul makes about itself, isn't it? It's not just letters. And I've noticed—I noticed it the first time I heard a devil talk, in my first life, on a beach, and I've never stopped noticing—a soul *sings* the way its name sounds. Not the name itself. The shape of it. Whole souls close their phrases. They come down to rest at the end, like a door settling on its hinge. Sundered souls don't. They stop. Every phrase just *stops*, like someone's cut the string."

The room was quiet.

"So listen," said Ship. "Every one of the four hundred and eleven."

He played the old woman's phrase. It rose, wavered, and came down to rest on a low note that sounded like the end of something. He played the boy's. It came down to rest. He played the bad singer's. Flat, but it came to rest.

"All of them. All whole. These are real people. They are the dead of that world, the ones who chose to wait for a god who was never coming back." His voice wobbled, very slightly. "And then there's this. It was under all of them. You can't hear it unless you take everything else away."

He took everything else away.

One voice was left. It was deep and smooth and it was singing the tune a beat ahead of the choir, the way a conductor breathes before the orchestra plays. It rose and fell. And at the end of every phrase it did not come to rest. It stopped dead, mid-air, like a dropped stitch.

Pell had his arms wrapped around himself.

"Sundered," said Hadrin.

"Sundered," said Ship. "It's leading them. They're singing its song."

"There you are, then," said Reva. "That's proof."

"No," said Hadrin. "It isn't."

Everyone looked at him. He took his spectacles off.

"I'm sorry. It is very beautiful work, and I believe every note of it. But a god's soul is sundered too. Ascension cuts the seal. If you play that to a woman who thinks her god has come home, she'll say, 'Yes, of course his voice has no seal. He's a god.' You'll have proved the thing is sundered. You won't have proved it's a fiend. Gods and fiends sound the same."

Ship said nothing. The walls were silent. After a moment, very quietly, a single harp string sounded, and stopped.

"Sorry," said Hadrin again, more gently. "It's half of it. It's a very good half."

Galen had been looking at the table. Now he looked up.

"What's the other half?"

"The other half," said Hadrin, "is the clock." He put his spectacles back on and unrolled a long sheet of paper covered in tiny figures. "I've had most of a day with the debris. Two hundred measurements. Distance along the tail, drift, rate of crumbling of the larger fragments. Three hundred and forty-one years, Captain. Plus or minus four. That god died on its home plane, because nothing else strips a home plane's barrier, and a god killed on its home plane is gone. Not banished. Not sleeping. *Gone*. Ammaduin isn't coming home because there's no Ammaduin to come."

"So the thing out there is lying," said Reva.

"The thing out there is lying, and its voice has no seal, which means it's a sundered soul that isn't a god. That makes it a fiend." He rolled the paper up. "Ship's half proves it's sundered. My half proves it isn't a god. Together they're a proof. Separately, they're a nice song and a lot of arithmetic."

"Can a woman on that plane check your arithmetic?" said Galen.

Hadrin considered.

"If they've kept records for three hundred years. If someone wrote down the day the sky broke."

Galen stood. His head brushed the beams. Kono, in the rafters, scrambled out of the way.

"Then that's who we need," he said. "Pell. Is the totem ready?"

"Tomorrow afternoon. Making a totem's slow."

"High tide's tomorrow," said Reva. "The recording said high tide."

"Then we're not shifting." Galen looked at the walls. "Ship. How do you feel about making an entrance?"

The harp in the walls played four bars of something so triumphant it was nearly rude.

"Oh Captain, my Captain," said Ship, "I thought you'd never ask."

## VII. The Golden Door

The away team, Team Rowan, gathered in the great hall at dawn. Galen named his away teams after trees. Ask him why and he'd tell you trees made good teams: roots in the ground, branches in the weather, nobody moving faster than they need to. Team Rowan was Galen, Hadrin, Zahra, and Kono, who had not been invited and was coming anyway.

"You're mascot," said Zahra. "You stay aboard."

"I'm morale," said Kono. "Morale goes where it's needed."

"She hunts rats," Galen said. "Let's hope it doesn't come to that." He checked his earpiece: a little silver whorl like a shell. Through it he could hear Reva on the bridge, Pell in the workshop, and Ship everywhere.

"Earpieces are live," said Reva in his ear. "Saltreach is the town in the recording. Drone puts it on a tidal estuary at the mouth of a big river. There's a square in the middle with a bell-frame and no bell. Tower to the north, on a spit of rock out in the water. That's where the drone heard the woman."

"Hesper," said Pell. "The drone heard someone call her Hesper. And she called the girl Lira."

"The tower," said Galen. "Ship, put us in the square. I want to talk to people before I talk to her. If we walk in on her at the bell with weapons out, we're the villains."

"Square it is," said Ship. "Stand back, please. Further. Further than that, Hadrin. I'm going to do the big one."

Hadrin, muttering, stepped back.

A point of light appeared in the middle of the great hall, a yard off the stone floor.

It hissed. The hiss started like a drop of water on a hot pan and grew, rising, until it was the sound of a kettle, of a forge-hose, of surf drawing back over shingle. The point stretched. It became a line of white-gold fire as tall as Galen, then taller, and the line split open down its length and peeled apart into a rectangle of light so bright that Zahra put her hand up over her eyes. The rectangle widened. It grew panels. It grew a lintel and a frame, and carvings crawled up the frame like ivy—waves, curling waves, and fish leaping between them, and at the top a sea-elf with a harp, very small, very proud of himself. The light cooled from white to gold. The door stood twelve feet tall in the great hall, gleaming, with a brass ring for a knocker shaped like a sea-serpent biting its tail, and from somewhere behind the walls a trumpet, which Ship did not have, played a fanfare.

"You've put yourself on it," said Hadrin.

"Just a little one."

"You've put yourself on the door."

"I'm very small. You'd hardly notice."

Kono had her nose pressed to the carvings. "Does every door you make have the fish?"

"Every one. The fish are my trademark."

"I love the fish," said Kono, with feeling.

The door swung open, slowly, on its own. Air came through: cold, wet, smelling of salt and mud and woodsmoke and fish, real fish, the kind that are dead and on a slab. Through the doorway was grey early light and wet cobbles and a ring of houses with steep slate roofs.

And people. Thirty or forty of them, in the middle of a market morning, frozen in place and staring, as a twelve-foot golden door opened out of nothing in the middle of their square with a sound like the sea.

A woman dropped a basket of eels. They slithered off across the stones. Nobody went after them.

Galen ducked through first. He had to; the door was tall but he was taller, and he had long ago learned that the first thing people saw should be the biggest and slowest-moving thing in the room. He stepped onto the cobbles, straightened up to his full nine feet, and held up both empty hands.

The crowd took a step back as one.

"Good morning," he said. "My name is Galen. We've come a long way and we mean no harm. I'm sorry about your eels."

The earpiece turned his words into theirs. He saw it land, saw the shock of understanding go through them. Then Zahra came through behind him with her glaive held point-down and her horns catching the light, and a boy at the front screamed, and Galen thought, *ah.*

Then Hadrin came through with his spectacles and his satchel, looking cross, and then Kono shot through over all their heads like a thrown jewel, did a loop in the air, landed on the empty bell-frame in the middle of the square, flared her gossamer wings in the dawn, and said, loudly, in their language, "*Hello!*"

The boy stopped screaming.

Behind them, the golden door swung shut, folded into a rectangle of light and a line and a single point, hissed once like a doused coal, and was gone.

In the silence, an old man came forward through the crowd. He was tall and stooped with long grey hair tied back with a leather cord, and he carried a stick topped with a little bronze bell that didn't ring, because it had no clapper. He smelled—Galen breathed in—of wet stone and cut rushes. Lawful neutral, steady, a little afraid.

"I'm Osric Dune," the old man said. "Bellwarden of Saltreach." His eyes went up, and up, to Galen's face. "Are you from him?"

"From whom?"

"From the god." The old man's voice cracked. "He said there'd be signs before the tide."

Galen looked down at him for a long moment.

"No," he said. "But I think we need to talk about him."

## VIII. The Bell-Chronicle

They talked in the bellwarden's house, which was low and dark and smelled of peat smoke and drying fish and very old paper. Half the town had followed them there and was listening at the shutters. Galen didn't mind. He wanted them to hear.

The people of Saltreach called themselves the Ostrin, and their world Ostmere. They were tall and grey-eyed, with hair that went white young, and they spoke their own language slowly, as if every word cost something. Osric told the story the way people tell stories they've told all their lives.

Once there was a god. His name was Ammaduin, and he lived in the white moon, and he had made the Ostrin out of salt and river-mud to keep him company. When an Ostrin died, they went up to the moon to live with him. The priests rang the great bells and the god answered, and there was healing in the answer, and rain when it was needed.

Then one night the moon broke.

"The Nightfall," said Osric. "The sky cracked and the white moon bled stones. And after that the bells were quiet. Nobody answered. The priests prayed until they died, and nobody answered them either." He touched the clapperless bell on his stick. "We took the clappers out after a hundred years. It seemed kinder than ringing them."

"And the dead?" said Galen.

"We sing them out over the water. We tell them, go to the moon. But the moon's broken." The old man looked at his hands. "For three hundred years we've been telling them to go somewhere that isn't there."

Hadrin had gone very still.

"Two years ago," Osric went on, "the singing started. At night, from the sky. People heard their mothers. Their sons. Every family in Saltreach has someone up there. And this spring, a new voice. *His* voice, under all of them. He said he had come home. He said the moon was broken, but he could make a new one if we let him in. He said he needs a bell to be rung at high tide, on the old Watchtower, where the first priests rang the first bell. And the dead will come home." His voice dropped. "He chose Hesper Vane to ring it. She's the lamplighter. She keeps the tide-tables. Her girl drowned two years ago, out past the spit. Lira. Eleven years old."

Zahra closed her eyes.

"You said the moon broke," said Hadrin, carefully. "Did anyone write down when?"

Osric looked at him as if he'd asked whether anyone had bothered to breathe.

"We are a people of bells," he said. "We keep a chronicle. Every ringing for nine hundred years. Every death. Every tide." He went to a chest by the hearth and opened it, and lifted out a book bound in grey sealskin, as thick as Galen's forearm, swollen with damp. "The Nightfall is on the four hundred and twelfth page. People still read it aloud on the anniversary."

"How long ago?" said Hadrin.

Osric turned the pages with a finger. Read. Counted something on his knuckles.

"Three hundred and forty-two winters," he said. "Next month."

Hadrin took his spectacles off. His hands were not quite steady.

"Captain," he said quietly, in Common, though the earpieces would carry it to everyone anyway. "Three hundred and forty-one, plus or minus four. That's the god's death. Their book and my arithmetic agree to the year."

"Tell him," said Galen. "Tell him all of it. Slowly."

Hadrin told him.

He told it the way a teacher would, because under the spectacles and the bad temper he had been one, once. He told Osric about home planes and barriers. That a god's barrier is powered by the god, and goes out only if the god is destroyed on its own plane. That the broken moon was a home plane, and its cracking was the god dying, and the stones that fell were the decay. That a god killed at home does not come back. Not in three hundred years, not in three thousand. And then he took a thing out of his satchel that Galen hadn't seen him pack: a fist-sized chunk of white stone, veined with gold, crumbling at one edge.

"Ship sent a second drone into the debris yesterday. It brought back this," he said. "It's a piece of your moon."

Osric took it in both hands. Through the shutters, someone made a sound like a sob.

"Then who," said the old man, "is singing?"

From behind the walls, very faintly, Galen heard the tide-tune start, high in the sky outside. The choir. Coming in with the morning. And under it—if you knew to listen—a voice a beat ahead, that never came to rest.

"Where is the Watchtower?" said Galen, standing. His head hit the ceiling beam. He didn't notice.

"On the spit," said Osric. "Captain. The tide turns within the hour."

## IX. Mister Hum

Aboard the *Tranelis Selevarun*, Kono's rat tally was about to become irrelevant.

Pell was the one who noticed. He was alone in the workshop, finishing the totem: the salt, the reeds, the bell-bronze, the silvered driftwood, fused now into a small grey disc that smelled of the estuary and fit the palm of his hand. He was humming. He hummed when he worked. And he stopped humming, and the humming went on.

He took his goggles off.

It was coming from the wall behind his workbench. A low, smooth, wordless hum, just below hearing, the way a boiler sounds two rooms away. He put his ear to the stone. Cold. The hum was inside it.

"Ship," he said. "Is that you?"

"Is what me?"

"The humming."

A pause. "Pell. I'm not humming."

The stone under Pell's ear was getting warm.

He stepped back. The hum rose in pitch. A crack ran up the wall behind his workbench, a thin, black, hairline crack, and through it, very faintly, came a smell like burnt hair. Then something thin and pale and many-jointed, like a white root, or a finger, pushed through.

"*SHIP*—"

"I see it." Ship's voice was suddenly everywhere, loud, and the walls rang with it. "Commander! Something's coming through the hull. Workshop, drone bay, and—three—four places. It's the thing from the haze. It's *boarding* me."

"Ships don't have a barrier," said Reva, on the bridge, already moving. "Oh, *damn* it. Zahra's planetside. Ship, get everyone with a weapon to the—where's it heading?"

"Everywhere. It's—" Ship's voice cut off and came back. "Commander. It's going for the phylactery."

For a moment nobody breathed.

Every ensouled ship's soul lived in a phylactery. Tranelis's was a sea-green crystal the size of a goose egg, set in a bronze housing behind the captain's chair, where it caught the scryer's light and threw little ripples of it on the ceiling. Smash it, and you didn't kill a ship. You took its soul out of the hull. And a soul loose in the astral sea, without a body, without a god, was exactly the kind of thing a fiend wanted.

"It wants me in the choir," said Ship, very quietly. "Doesn't it."

In the workshop, Pell picked up a wrench. Then he put it down and picked up something better: a crossbow he'd been building as a hobby, with a crank and a little brass magazine and bolts with alchemical heads. It was an artificer's toy. It was going to have to do.

The white finger pushed further through the wall. Another joined it.

Then something small and iridescent dropped out of the rafters like a stone, landed on the finger, and bit it.

The finger convulsed and withdrew with a sound like a scream played backwards. Kono hit the floor, spat, and looked up at Pell with black blood on her teeth.

"What are you doing here?" said Pell. "You went down with the away team!"

"I came back. Asked Ship for a little door when the old man got his book out. Books are boring." She flicked her tail. "Rats, Sprocket. I've been chasing rats in this ship for five weeks. I know where the walls are thin." She glared at the crack. "And *that* is a very big rat."

"That's a fiend."

"It's a very big rat that *hums*," said Kono. "Mister Hum. I'm calling it Mister Hum." She took off, wings blurring. "This way. It's coming in through the grove. It's the only bit of the hull with soil instead of stone. Follow me!"

Pell grabbed his crossbow and followed her.

Above them, in the walls, Ship started to play.

It was *Orlalassar's Battle Theme*. Galen had heard it a dozen times in training and it had never once failed to make him want to run at something. Drums came first, hard and fast, out of the stone. Then the horns, then a fiddle line going up and up like a bird trying to get over a wall, and under it all a sea-elf's harp, driving, relentless, and Pell, running down a corridor with a crossbow behind a faerie dragon, found he was grinning so hard his face hurt.

On the ship's tavern level, Senior Crewmate Odo Barrowmint, a crewdwarf who had been a pit-fighter before he was a cook, put down his ladle and picked up his cleaver.

## X. The Watchtower

The Watchtower stood on a spit of black rock a quarter-mile out into the estuary, joined to the shore by a causeway the tide was already lapping over. Galen ran it, ankle-deep in cold water. Zahra ran beside him. Hadrin, shorter in the leg, was falling behind and swearing about it.

The sky above Saltreach was grey and low and full of singing.

It had grown, as the tide grew. The whole sky had it now. You couldn't tell where the voices came from; they came from everywhere, from the clouds and the water and the stones under your feet. Hundreds of them, the tide-tune, slow and aching. People had come out onto the shore to listen. Some were crying. Some were singing back.

And under it, a beat ahead, smooth and enormous, the voice that never came to rest.

"*Ring it, Hesper. It is time. Ring it, and I can come in, and they can come home.*"

"Ship," Galen panted. "Ship, talk to me."

No answer. Only, very faintly through the earpiece, drums.

"Reva?"

"It's aboard." Reva's voice was clipped and calm, the voice of a merchant captain with pirates on deck. "It came through the hull as soon as you left. Kono's with Pell. Odo's got a cleaver. Ship's—busy. It wants the phylactery, Captain. It wants him in the choir."

Galen's stride didn't break, but something in his chest did.

"Keep him safe," he said.

"Working on it. Go save your mother."

The Watchtower was old, older than anything in the town, built of the same black rock it stood on. Its door hung open. Inside, a stair spiralled up into dimness, and from the top of the stair came a sound: metal on metal, a hammer. Someone was fitting a clapper.

They climbed. Galen went sideways, his shoulders scraping both walls, ducking under every turn of the stair. The tower smelled of salt and guano and lamp oil. And something else, getting stronger as he went up: rain on slate, which was neutral good, a decent person, a tired one. And curling through it, thin and sour, like milk just on the turn, something else.

It had been rain on slate a few days ago, Galen thought. It wouldn't be much longer.

At the top was a single round room open to the sky on all sides, with a roof held up by eight black pillars. In the middle hung a bell. It was enormous, green-bronze and old, mended in a dozen places, and at its lip a broken chip of metal was missing, the size of a palm—the same size as the curl of bronze in Pell's totem. A woman was on a ladder beside it, with a hammer, fixing a new clapper into place. Wind came through the open sides and pulled at her hair, which was white, like all the Ostrin, though she couldn't have been forty.

She heard them. She turned.

She was tall, and thin with grief, and she had a lamplighter's long pole slung across her back and tide-tables inked on the inside of her left forearm in small neat columns. Her eyes went to Galen's face, and up, and up.

"You're the ones from the door," said Hesper Vane. "The golden door. The whole town saw."

"We are."

"He said you'd try to stop me." She didn't come down the ladder. Her hand tightened on the hammer. "He said you were liars from the dark. He said you'd say anything."

"*Ring it, Hesper,*" said the sky. "*Ring it, my child. They are lying. They want your daughter for themselves.*"

Zahra had her glaive half-raised. Galen put out a hand without looking at her, and after a moment, she lowered it.

"I'm not going to stop you," he said.

Zahra looked at him. Hadrin, wheezing at the top of the stairs, looked at him.

"I could," Galen said. "I'm nine feet tall and I've got a holy oath and a sword I haven't drawn. I could take that hammer off you and carry you down the stairs, and you'd hate me until the end of your life, and you'd be right to. And next high tide, he'd find someone else." He sat down on the floor, cross-legged, which put his head about level with hers on the ladder. It was the most disarming thing he knew how to do. "I'm not going to stop you. I'm going to ask you to check something. And then you decide."

Hesper stared at him.

"Check what?"

"Your tide-tables are on your arm," said Galen. "You keep the times of the water. You keep records. Osric says you've read the bell-chronicle more than anyone in Saltreach."

"I've read it cover to cover twice." Defensive. "There's nothing else to do at night when your daughter's singing to you from the sky."

"Then you know how long ago the moon broke."

"Three hundred and forty-two years next month. Everyone knows that."

"Hadrin," said Galen. "Show her."

Hadrin came forward. He held out the chunk of white stone, and a sheet of paper covered in his tiny figures, and he looked very uncomfortable to be holding them in front of a woman on a ladder with a hammer.

"Before I knew your world existed," he said, "before I knew your name, I measured the broken moon. How far the stones have spread. How fast they crumble. And I got three hundred and forty-one years, plus or minus four." He held up the paper. "I'm not asking you to trust me. Here are my numbers. You understand rates. You understand how far a tide goes in an hour. You can check every line."

She didn't take it. But she looked at it.

"*Lies,*" said the sky. "*Numbers are lies. I am Ammaduin. I was sleeping, and now I am awake.*"

"There's no sleeping," said Galen gently. "That's the trouble. A god whose moon breaks has been killed in its own house. And a god killed in its own house doesn't sleep. It doesn't come back. When the barrier around your moon went out, that was your god going out, all at once, forever. That's what the Nightfall was."

Hesper's face had gone very white.

"Then who is he?"

"Ship," said Galen. "Can you give me thirty seconds?"

For a long moment there was nothing in his ear but drums, very loud.

Then the drums stopped.

"*Oh Captain, my Captain.*" Ship sounded out of breath, which was impossible, since he did not breathe. "I can give you thirty seconds. I'm not promising thirty-one."

## XI. The Grove

In the druid grove, under a ceiling of enchanted daylight, a fiend was coming up through the soil.

The grove was the one room aboard the *Tranelis Selevarun* without a stone floor. It had a stand of young birches, a pond, a little meadow, and three feet of good black earth on top of the hull. Kono had been right: it was the soft place. The white fingers had found it. Now there was a whole hand, and an arm, too many joints in it, and a shoulder coming up out of the mud like a corpse out of a bog, and that smell, burnt hair and rotten fruit, so thick in the warm air that Pell's eyes streamed.

He'd emptied the crossbow's magazine into it. The alchemical bolts had burst in bright green flares. The thing had shuddered at each one, and kept coming.

Odo Barrowmint had hit it with the cleaver twice, which had done more. He was down now, by the pond, with Sister Abelone, the ship's Chief Religionist—a halfling in a blue habit who smelled of chamomile—kneeling beside him with her hands glowing gold. He'd live. He was cursing in three languages, which was a good sign.

Reva stood between the thing and the corridor that led to the bridge, with a rapier in one hand and a dagger in the other and her jaw set. Kono hovered over her head, spitting.

"What does it want?" Pell gasped. He was reloading with shaking hands. "Ship, what does it *want*?"

"*You know what I want,*" said the thing in the soil, in the warm god-voice, very calmly. "*The singer. Such a voice, and nobody to sing for. Let me take him to the choir, little gnome, and I will leave the rest of you be.*"

"Not a chance," said Reva.

"*I was not asking you.*"

And then the voice came from everywhere at once: from the walls, from the ceiling, from the birch trees and the pond. Ship.

"Pell. Kono. Commander. I need thirty seconds of my own voice for the captain, and while I'm doing it I can't play. And it's the music that's been slowing it down. Did you notice? Every time I played the theme it flinched. It's been trying to *sing over me* the whole time. That's why it's slow. It can't climb and conduct at the same time and I'm making it fight to keep the tune."

Pell's eyes went wide.

"Then I need someone else to play," Ship said. "Anyone. Anything. It doesn't have to be good. It just has to be *loud*, and it has to be *not its song*."

Silence in the grove, for one heartbeat.

Then Kono landed on the edge of the pond, puffed her chest out to twice its normal size, and began, at the top of her small lungs, to sing a song about a sailor, a mermaid, and a misunderstanding involving a barrel of pickled herring.

It was one of the bawdy songs from Ship's tavern. It was very, very rude. Kono had clearly been listening to it every night for five weeks.

Odo Barrowmint, still flat on his back, started bellowing along on the chorus. Sister Abelone went pink to the ears and kept her hands glowing. Pell, who didn't know the words, picked up a trowel and a watering can and started banging them together.

The thing in the soil froze. The arm stopped moving. The voice that never came to rest tried to start the tide-tune, and couldn't find the beat, because there was a faerie dragon six feet away singing about what the mermaid did with the herring, and a dwarf who was louder than she was.

"*Thank you,*" said Ship, in the walls. "*That's dreadful. I love it. Thirty seconds.*"

## XII. Two Halves of a Proof

"Hesper," said Ship.

His voice came out of the earpieces of Team Rowan, out of the air of the tower room, out of nowhere, warm and very gentle. It was the first time Hesper had heard him. She looked around wildly.

"That's Ship," said Galen. "He's who made the golden door. He's a bard. Listen to him."

"I've spent a day listening to your choir," said Ship. "I want to play you something. Then I want you to tell me what you hear. You don't have to believe me. Just listen."

He played her a voice.

It came out of the air of the tower, out of the bell, out of the black stones: a girl's voice, eleven years old, singing the tide-tune. Alone. No choir behind it. Thin and sweet and a little off-key on the high notes, the way children are.

Hesper Vane dropped the hammer.

It clattered down off the ladder and bounced on the floor and nobody looked at it.

"That's her," she whispered. "That's Lira. That's—that's how she always went flat on—"

"I know," said Ship. "Listen to how she finishes."

The girl's phrase rose, and wavered, and came down to rest, soft, on a low note, like a door settling closed.

"Everyone in your sky does that," Ship said. "Every one of the four hundred and eleven. Every one of your dead. It's how whole souls sing. It's in the shape of their true names. They come to rest." He paused. "Now listen to *him*."

He took the girl away. He played the other voice. The deep smooth beautiful voice that was a beat ahead, singing the same phrase.

It rose. And at the end, where Lira's voice had come home, it stopped. Cut off, mid-air. A dropped stitch.

Hesper was very still on the ladder.

"That's a sundered soul," said Hadrin quietly. "No seal. Now, a god's soul is sundered too, so that alone doesn't prove anything. But your god's been dead for three hundred and forty-one years. Your book says so and the stones say so. So it isn't a god. And if a sundered soul isn't a god—"

"It's a fiend," said Hesper.

Nobody said anything.

She was looking down at her arm. At the tide-tables, in their small neat columns. Galen watched her lips move. She was counting.

"Three hundred and forty-two winters next month," she said at last. "Plus or minus four." She looked at Hadrin's paper, still in his hand. "Give me that."

He climbed onto the bottom of the ladder and handed it up.

She read it. Galen watched her read it. She read it the way he had seen engineers read plans, with her finger, line by line, lips moving. Twice she stopped and frowned, and once she said "*oh*," and checked something against the inside of her arm. It took a long time. The tide was nearly up. The sky was singing.

"*Ring it,*" said the sky. It wasn't calm anymore. "*Ring it now. Ring it, Hesper, and your daughter will come home.*"

Hesper folded the paper and put it in her coat.

"My daughter," she said, "is up there. Singing for a liar." She came down the ladder. "And he's had her for two years. Hasn't he."

"Not *had*," said Galen. "She's a whole soul. They all are. He can't own them. He doesn't know their true names; nobody does. He's just told them a lie they wanted to believe, the same lie he told you. Come home. Your god's back. Sing, and the door will open." He stood up, carefully, and his head brushed the roof. "But they can choose. If they knew. If someone told them, in a way they could check."

Hesper looked at the bell.

Then she looked at Galen, and her face did something complicated.

"Can your ship sing to them?" she said.

## XIII. The Door in the Sky

The grove was a mess. The birches were broken, the pond was black, and Odo had lost his voice. But the thing in the soil had stopped climbing, and as Ship came back to himself in the walls and picked up his harp again, it began, very slowly, to draw its arm back down.

"*Oh no,*" said Ship, cheerfully. "*No, you don't.*"

He let the battle theme drop. He started something else.

It was the tide-tune. The Ostrin dead's own song, the one the choir had been singing in the sky for two years. Ship had listened to it for a day and a night and taken it apart voice by voice, and now he put it back together, but in his own way. He played it on the harp, slow, and he sang over it in his warm tenor in the Ostrin language, the earpieces translating as he went. And the words he sang were not the words of the choir.

Galen heard it in his earpiece, at the top of the Watchtower. Hesper heard it. And far above them, a faint answering murmur went through the haze.

*The moon broke three hundred and forty-two years ago,* Ship sang. *Count it. You were there. Some of you were there.*

*A god killed in his house doesn't come home.*

*Listen to the one who conducts you. Listen to how he ends his phrases.*

*Then listen to how you end yours.*

And he sang the two endings, the one that came to rest and the one that didn't, over and over, the way you'd teach a child the difference between two notes.

"Ship," said Reva, on the bridge. "Is it working?"

"*I don't know,*" said Ship. "*They're listening. They're—Commander. They're stopping.*"

In the sky above Saltreach, the tide-tune faltered.

One voice dropped out. Then ten. Then a hundred. Galen, at the open side of the tower, looked up and saw the grey clouds over the estuary and heard them going quiet, a wave of quiet rolling across the sky, voice by voice by voice, until there was only one left. Deep and smooth and enormous and singing alone, a beat ahead of a choir that wasn't there anymore.

It sounded, all by itself, like what it was. Like something that had never once come to rest.

Then it stopped.

"Captain," said Ship, very fast. "It's let go of the hull. It's pulling out. All of it. It's going back to the haze. It's going to—"

"It's going to try and run," said Reva. "Or try and *take* them. By force, if it can't by lies."

"Can it?" said Galen.

"Not by the rules," said Hadrin. "They're sovereign. But it's a demon's harbinger. It doesn't care about rules. And if it can't take them, it'll run."

"Where?"

"Home," said Hadrin. "Probably. If we hurt it badly enough, it'll be banished there anyway. And that's a problem."

Galen understood him a moment late. Then he understood it all at once, and his stomach dropped.

A harbinger was a seed. A demon scattered thousands of them into the dark, and most of them found nothing, ever. The demon didn't know which ones had found something. It only knew when somebody summoned it. If the harbinger ran home, or was killed and banished home, it would bring the knowledge with it: here is a world, with a barrier and no god, with three hundred years of dead around it and a people who will ring a bell if you sing to them sweetly enough.

And the demon would come with an anchor.

"We can't kill it," said Galen. "And we can't let it go."

"Captain," said Reva. "I think that's what's called a dilemma."

Galen stood at the top of the Watchtower with the wind in his beard and the sea coming up over the causeway below, and thought.

"Hadrin," he said. "A harbinger's built to live out in the open sea. It moves by will?"

"Like everything else out there. Except organics."

"And there's a place, two days back, where will doesn't move anything."

There was a long silence on the channel.

Then, in Galen's earpiece, very softly, a harp began to play a few bright notes of something that sounded like a door opening.

"*Oh Captain,*" said Ship. "*Oh Captain, my Captain. Do you want the big one?*"

"I want the biggest one you've ever made."

"*Shift me the away team first. Pell's finished the totem. And Hesper—*"

"I'm coming," said Hesper Vane. She had her lamplighter's pole in one hand. She was white to the lips. "I'm coming. That's my daughter up there."

---

They came aboard by shifting, which was a soft thing, a blink and a lurch and the smell of salt marsh giving way to the smell of stone. Then they ran for the bridge.

All the stations were lit. The full complement was at them: Reva at the first officer's station, Hadrin at arcana, Pell at the tinker's bench with his singed hair standing straight up, Zahra at security, Hesper standing behind the captain's chair with her hands knotted together. Kono was on the back of the chair, hoarse. Galen took his seat.

In the scryer, the harbinger hung in the silver, and it was not pretending any more.

It had stopped being a tall robed man. It was something like a white tree turned upside down and something like a jellyfish and something like nothing at all, a mass of pale many-jointed limbs trailing through the dark. And it was reaching. Around it the haze had broken up, the dead scattering like startled fish, and it was reaching into them, grabbing at them, and wherever it caught one there was a thin high sound that was the worst thing Galen had ever heard.

"It can't hold them," said Ship. "They're slipping through. They know now. But it's trying."

"Can you reach it?"

"Yes. Hold on to something."

Galen held on to the arms of his chair.

Out in the astral sea, between the *Tranelis Selevarun* and the dead and the fiend, a point of light appeared.

It hissed. It hissed so loud that the sound came through the hull and through the stone and through the soles of their boots. Hesper flinched and put her hands over her ears. The point stretched into a line, and the line was a mile long. It split, and peeled apart into a rectangle so tall that the scryer couldn't hold all of it; Ship had to pull the image back, and back, and back. The light swelled and shaped itself. A lintel. A frame. Panels as big as fields. And up the frame, over a mile of white-gold light, the carvings crawled, climbing, curling: waves, and leaping fish, thousands of them, each one perfect, and at the very top a tiny sea-elf with a harp.

The door cooled to gold. It hung there in the astral sea, enormous, impossible, with a brass ring the size of a town for a knocker. It was the most beautiful thing Galen had ever seen, and the most showing-off.

"You put yourself on it again," said Hadrin weakly.

"*I'm very small,*" said Ship. "*You'd hardly notice.*"

Beside Galen, Hesper Vane was crying, silently, with her hands pressed to her mouth.

The door swung open.

On the other side was the Glass. It looked like nothing: a patch of the astral sea that lay flat and still and perfectly smooth, the light lying on it like a pane, nothing moving. The door opened onto its very middle.

"Now what?" said Zahra. "It's not going to walk through politely."

"No," said Ship. "But it's a harbinger, and I'm a ship, and ships don't have barriers. It's been trying to get *inside* me all morning." The harp in the walls played a single sly note. "So I'm going to let it."

"Ship—" said Reva.

"Trust me, Commander."

The *Tranelis Selevarun* moved.

She went towards the harbinger at full will, fast, straight at it. The fiend saw her coming. It turned from the scattering dead and opened, all its many-jointed limbs flaring wide, and it reached for her with everything it had, for the hull and the soil of the grove and the bright sea-green phylactery behind the captain's chair that it wanted so badly—

—and Ship, at the last instant, swerved.

He put the golden door between them.

The harbinger went through it reaching. It went through it at full speed, with all the will it had, and on the other side was the Glass, where will moved nothing at all. It hit the flat silver like a bird hitting a window. And it stopped.

It hung there in the middle of the becalming. Its limbs flailed. Nothing happened. It pushed with all the will it had, and the Glass let it push, and it did not move one inch.

"There," said Ship softly, and shut the door.

The door swung closed, slowly, a mile of gold in the dark. It folded. It became a rectangle of light, and a line a mile long, and a point. The point hissed once, like a coal dropped in the sea.

Then it was gone.

## XIV. What the Dead Chose

They didn't say anything for a long time.

Out in the astral sea, the haze was drifting back together. Slowly, uncertainly, the way a shoal does after a shark has gone by. Ship had stopped playing. In the silence, very faintly, they could hear the dead talking among themselves. Not singing. Talking.

"How long will it be in there?" said Pell, at last.

"Until it drifts out," said Hadrin. "Which could be a year. Could be a thousand. Nobody knows how big the Glass really is, or which way it drifts. Ship put it in the middle." He took his spectacles off and rubbed his eyes. "It's not forever. But it's a very long time, for a seed. And nobody at home knows where it went."

"Note it in the log," said Galen. "For whoever comes after us. If it ever drifts out, someone needs to be waiting."

Hesper was at the scryer. She had not taken her eyes off the haze.

"Can I talk to her?" she said.

Galen looked at Ship's walls.

"*Yes,*" said Ship. "*If she wants to. I can open a sending. It won't be long. Twenty-five words, and twenty-five back.*"

Hesper didn't answer for a while. Then she said, "Twenty-five words," in a voice like breaking glass. "After two years."

"*I'll give you mine,*" said Ship. "*All the sendings I can cast today. I can make it a conversation.*"

She nodded, and couldn't speak.

They left her alone at the scryer. Galen sent everyone off the bridge except himself, and he went and sat in his ready room with the door half-closed, and did not listen, and heard anyway. He heard the soft chime of the spell opening. He heard Hesper say a name. He heard, out of the air, a thin girl's voice that went a little flat on the high notes.

He heard Hesper laugh, once. Then cry, then laugh again.

After a long while, the spells ran out.

When Galen came back out, Hesper was sitting on the floor beside the scryer with her knees pulled up. Kono was curled in her lap, and Hesper was stroking her without seeming to know she was doing it. Ship was playing *Oribella's Theme*, very softly, the one with the sad thing underneath it.

"She's going to end," said Hesper. "She says she's tired. She says most of them are. They were waiting for something that was never coming, and she's tired of waiting." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "She asked if that was all right. With me."

Galen lowered himself to the floor beside her, slowly, because the floor was a long way down.

"Is it?" he said.

Hesper thought about it for a long time.

"No," she said. "But it's hers."

Below, in the silver, the haze was thinning. Not scattering. Thinning. One light and then another going out, gently, like lamps at the end of a long night, and some staying. Some would stay a while, Galen thought. Some would want to watch over the people they'd left. That was their choice too. The Federation would have to think hard about how to keep them safe out there, and he would put that in the log as well.

One small light, near the edge of the haze, flared bright for a moment, very bright, and then went out.

Hesper didn't make a sound. Kono, in her lap, put her small iridescent head against Hesper's wrist and closed her eyes.

Ship let *Oribella's Theme* come to its end. It came to rest on a low note, soft, like a door settling closed.

---

## XV. Epilogue: Captain's Log

The pen was too small for his hand. He wrote anyway.

> **Captain's log, stardate 1.713.**
>
> *First contact with the Ostrin of Ostmere, a single-race prime plane whose god, Ammaduin, was killed on his home plane approximately 342 years ago. His dead have been stranded in the astral sea since.*
>
> *We encountered a demon's harbinger posing as the dead god. It was attempting to induce a local, Hesper Vane, to perform a summoning disguised as a religious rite. The summoning was prevented. The harbinger was not killed. A harbinger killed or allowed to flee would likely carry knowledge of this plane back to the demon that made it. Instead, Ship trapped it in the Glass (see 1.712). Recommend the Glass be watched. If it ever drifts out, someone needs to be there.*
>
> *Ship proved the harbinger's nature by ear, from the shape of its singing; Lt Cmdr Morrowmere proved it by arithmetic, from the decay of the god's home plane. The Ostrin checked both against their own records and reached the same answer. I note, for Fleet's benefit, that I knew it was a fiend the moment I smelled it, and that this was of no use to anyone at all.*
>
> *Commendations: Lt Cmdr Morrowmere. Lt Fennimore. Senior Crewmate Barrowmint, who is recovering. Sister Abelone. And Kono, who for the official record sang a song I will not name in a log, at volume, at a fiend, and saved the ship.*
>
> *Ship has asked to be commended. Ship is commended.*
>
> *Many of the stranded dead have chosen to end. Some remain. They are sovereign, and it is not our place to choose for them, but they are unprotected out there, and I request guidance from Fleet on what we can offer them.*
>
> *Mrs. Vane has asked about the Federation. I told her the truth: that we are young, and far away, and we will come back. She said she would keep the tide-tables until we did.*
>
> *G. Underbough, Captain, A.F.S.* Tranelis Selevarun

He signed it, closed the book, and stowed it in the felt-lined compartment in the arm of his chair.

In the walls, Ship was playing something new. It had the tide-tune in it somewhere, slowed down and turned over. It came to rest at the end of every phrase.

"What's that one called?" said Galen.

The harp hesitated. Then, very pleased: "You *asked*."

"I asked."

"It doesn't have a name yet," said Ship. "I think it's going to be called *Hesper's Theme*. Do you want to hear it from the beginning? It's got a bit in the middle where the fish come in."

---

Four hundred leagues away and instantly, in the Hall of Records at Fleet Headquarters, Senior Crewmate Tibbet Quarry watched the blue ink bloom up out of the vellum, word after word, and forgot his tea.

When the ink reached *Ship is commended*, he laughed out loud again, alone among the lecterns. When it reached the part about the Glass, he stopped laughing and went to fetch the Admiral.

He checked the signature before he went. Large, careful, pressing hard on the downstrokes.

Steady. Good.

## 3. Story beats by plot

**A-plot: The false god.** A harbinger posing as the Ostrin's dead god is leading their stranded dead in song to get a grieving mother to ring the summoning bell; the crew must prove it a fiend and stop it without sending word home.

1. Section II, bridge. Galen, Reva, Ship, Kono. They find a living plane beside a dead god's home plane, and a haze around the living one; Ship hears a choir and his music stops.
2. Section III, briefing. Full senior staff. Hadrin explains the dead god and the stranded dead; the crew realizes the haze is three centuries of Ostrin dead, and asks who they're singing to.
3. Section IV, workshop. Galen, Hadrin, Pell. The drone records Hesper promising her daughter to ring the bell at high tide because "he" told her to.
4. Section V, bridge. Galen, Reva, Zahra, harbinger. The thing claims to be Ammaduin. Galen smells a fiend; Reva points out he can't prove it.
5. Section VI, briefing. Ship, Hadrin, Galen, Reva, Pell. Ship's ear shows the dead are whole and the conductor is sundered; Hadrin shows that's not enough, then supplies the decay-clock: the god died at home, so the conductor is a fiend.
6. Section VII, great hall to Saltreach. Team Rowan, Kono, villagers, Osric. The golden door; first contact.
7. Section VIII, Osric's house. The bell-chronicle dates the Nightfall to the year Hadrin computed; the town learns the god is dead, and the choir starts up as the tide turns.
8. Section X, Watchtower. Galen, Zahra, Hadrin, Hesper. Galen refuses to stop her by force and asks her to check the proof herself.
9. Section XII, Watchtower. Ship plays Lira alone, then the conductor alone; Hesper checks Hadrin's numbers line by line, concludes it's a fiend, and asks if Ship can sing to the dead.
10. Section XIII, Saltreach and space. Ship sings the proof to the choir in their own tune; the dead stop singing. The harbinger turns to force. Galen realizes killing it or letting it go both betray the plane, and remembers the Glass.
11. Section XIII, bridge. Full complement plus Hesper. Ship opens a mile-tall golden door onto the Glass, baits the harbinger with himself, swerves, and it flies through into the becalming and is stuck.

**B-plot: The boarding.** With the away team gone, the harbinger tries to take Ship's phylactery.

1. Section IX, workshop. Pell, Ship, Kono. Humming in the walls; a white finger through the hull. Kono, back aboard, bites it and leads Pell to the grove.
2. Section IX, ship-wide. Reva, Ship, Odo. Ship realizes it wants his soul for the choir; he plays *Orlalassar's Battle Theme*.
3. Section XI, grove. Pell, Kono, Reva, Odo, Abelone. The harbinger climbs through the grove's soil; Ship works out that his music slows it by fighting its song, and he needs thirty seconds for the captain.
4. Section XI, grove. Kono sings a bawdy tavern song at full volume; Odo and Pell join; the harbinger can't find its beat.
5. Section XIII, ship. Once its choir falls silent, the harbinger lets go of the hull and flees to the haze, setting up the A-plot climax.

**C-plot: Hesper and Lira.** A mother who has heard her drowned daughter singing for two years has to choose between a lie and a goodbye.

1. Section IV. The drone hears Hesper talking to Lira and admitting she's frightened.
2. Section VIII. Osric explains who Hesper is and why she was chosen.
3. Section X. Galen smells her goodness souring; she nearly rings the bell.
4. Section XII. She hears Lira's voice alone, sees that it comes to rest, checks the numbers, and decides for herself.
5. Section XIII. She insists on coming aboard to see it through.
6. Section XIV. Ship gives her all his sendings; Lira chooses to end; Hesper accepts it ("But it's hers"). Ship ends *Oribella's Theme* on a resting note.
7. Section XV. Ship names a new song *Hesper's Theme*.

**Frame: The logbook.** Section I and Section XV, with Tibbet Quarry at Fleet HQ reading the twin book; the first log plants the Glass, the last one requests that it be watched.

**How they intersect.** The B-plot and A-plot pivot on the same fact: the harbinger can't conduct and act at once, so Kono's song on the ship buys the thirty seconds Ship needs to play the proof to Hesper on the planet. Hesper's decision (C) turns the proof (A) from something the crew knows into something the Ostrin know, which is what lets Ship sing it to the dead. The opening log's throwaway hazard becomes the climax's solution.
