# Astral Trek, Episode 1: "Measure Twice"

## I. Captain's Log

"Captain's log, stardate 1.512."

Captain Rhosyn Calder said it to the empty bridge the way she had once said *the court will come to order*: plainly, to no one in particular, trusting that the right party was listening.

The right party was. The ring of stations around her stood dark, the chairs pushed in, and at the center of the room the scryer turned slowly in its cradle, a sphere of clear crystal the size of a cart wheel, filled with the silver nothing of the astral sea. Somewhere behind the walls a ship was paying attention.

"Logged, Captain," said the ship. His voice came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, warm, with a sea-elf's lilt that still made her think of harbors. "Go on. I'm all ears. Well. All hull."

"Forty days out of Rosste on our first assignment. The *Tranelis Selevarun* has reached the charted coordinates of the prime plane Ondrevel, eighteen-forty, minus two-twelve, sixty-five. Fleet's entry comes from the organic ship *Grandmother Lisk*, who passed the plane nine years before the Founding and reported a single disc, lit by a small cold sun, inhabited by an owl-like people who build towers. No Federation vessel has visited since. Our orders are first contact: introduce ourselves, learn what they want, offer what we can."

She paused. The scryer turned. In it, the sea went on being silver and empty in every direction.

"We have arrived," she said, "and Ondrevel has not."

"For the record," said the ship, "I want it noted that I steered *perfectly*."

"That's not how logs work, Sel."

"It's how *mine* work. Addendum, in the ship's voice: the captain's navigation was impeccable, the navigator's sums were flawless, and the plane is simply rude. End addendum."

Rhosyn Calder was fifty-one years old. She had spent twenty of those years as a circuit magistrate on the river roads of Rosste, riding from town to town with a law book, a sword she rarely drew, and a gift she rarely used. She had heard every kind of testimony a person could give. She knew what it sounded like when a story had a hole in it.

This one had a plane-sized hole in it.

"Captain's log, continued," she said. "We are investigating. Calder out."

"Shall I wake the others?"

"Wake Lieutenant Ashgrove first," she said. "And tell her to bring every chart she owns."

"She owns rather a lot of charts."

"Then tell her to bring a crewmate."

## II. The Empty Sea

Lieutenant Pemberly Ashgrove did not believe in empty.

Empty was what you called a place before you had measured it properly. She stood at the navigator's station with three charts pinned under her small brown hands, a fourth clenched in her teeth, and her spectacles pushed so far up her forehead that they had become a kind of crown. She was a gnome, forty-four, and had been the youngest chart-clerk in the history of the Rosste Admiralty Archive before she had been its most annoying.

"It's not here," she said around the chart.

"We'd gathered," said Commander Tobin Marrow.

The first officer was a half-orc of enormous patience and enormous forearms, who had been a bosun on a helm-ship for fifteen years before the Fleet found him. He leaned against the rail with a mug of tea that looked like a thimble in his hand.

"No, I mean it's *really* not here." Pemmy spat out the chart and caught it. "If a plane's barrier fails, the plane decays. It breaks up. There'd be debris for leagues. Rock, dead trees, bits of tower. Sel, anything?"

"Nothing," said the ship. "I've swept a hundred leagues in every direction, and if there's a pebble out there, it's a very discreet pebble."

"So it wasn't destroyed," said Pemmy. "And it wasn't here. Or it *was* here, and it isn't now."

"Planes don't move," said Lieutenant Commander Sorrow Vey.

The Chief Arcanist sat at her station with her tail curled around the chair leg and her chin on her fist. She was a tiefling, with horns swept back like a ram's and the patient, faintly bored expression of a woman who had spent years explaining to people why their spells didn't work. "Things in the sea move by will. Ships, swimmers, us. A plane doesn't have a will. It just sits."

"Does it?" said Pemmy. "How would we know?"

There was a short silence on the bridge.

"Because they're on the charts," said Sorrow, slowly, "where they're supposed to be."

"Charts we started making five hundred and twelve days ago," said Pemmy. "From the memories of three organic ships who've been swimming around for centuries without ever writing anything down."

Something landed on the rail beside Tobin's elbow with a soft, heavy thump: a tabby cat the size of a small dog, with a torn ear, a white bib, and a pair of folded brown wings on his back. He settled his paws neatly, surveyed the bridge, and yawned so widely that his teeth showed.

"Oh, good," said Barnacle. "Sums is having a crisis. I love a crisis. Did anyone see a grey rat with a white foot go past here? About this long? Insolent?"

"No," said Rhosyn.

"He's been mocking me for three days. I've named him the Admiral." The tressym's yellow eyes flicked to the scryer. "Why is it empty? Where's the owl place? I was promised owls."

"That's what we're working on," said Tobin.

"Well, work faster, Bosun. I've been looking forward to owls for forty days. I have *questions* for owls."

"Pemmy," said Rhosyn. "Take me through it. Slowly. As if I were twelve."

Pemmy brightened. Nobody had asked her to go slowly in years. Usually they asked her to stop.

"Fleet's chart puts Ondrevel here," she said, tapping. "That position came from Grandmother Lisk, a founding organic, so nobody checked it. Why would you? She's a founding organic. She's as old as the Rosste mountains and twice as large." She slid the second chart over the first. "But the chart has an appendix: *unconfirmed sightings*. Things merchants and salvagers saw and reported but nobody verified. Mostly nonsense. Sea serpents. Lights. One drunk helm-caster who swore he saw a plane shaped like a teapot."

"Was it?" said Barnacle.

"Unconfirmed," said Pemmy. "*But.* Four years before the Founding, a salvage helm-ship out of Rosste called the *Pennywise* logged a sighting at twenty-twenty, minus ninety-two, five. Quote: lamp-lit disc, small cold sun, towers, owl-faced folk seen through the glass. End quote. It was filed as unconfirmed because it was in the wrong place."

"The wrong place," Rhosyn repeated.

"The wrong place by a hundred and eighty leagues along one axis, a hundred and twenty along another, and sixty along the third. Everybody assumed the *Pennywise*'s helm-caster was drunk, or her navigator couldn't add. And maybe she couldn't. But Captain..." Pemmy's voice went quick and bright. "It's the *same plane*. Small cold sun. Towers. Owls. Two witnesses, two different positions, five years apart."

Rhosyn folded her arms. Twenty years of court came back to her as if she'd never left it. *Two witnesses disagree about where. Neither disagrees about what.*

"When witnesses disagree on the where," she said, "they're usually agreeing on the what. And the what is the important part."

"Exactly!" Pemmy actually bounced. "So either both witnesses made mistakes, very large ones, that happen to describe the same plane perfectly, or..."

"Or the plane went from one place to the other," said Tobin.

"In five years," said Pemmy. "So if it kept going the same way at the same pace..." She was scribbling already, fast, on the margin of a chart that probably had not been meant for margins. "A hundred and eighty leagues in five thousand days is point-oh-three-six a day. A hundred and twenty is point-oh-two-four. Minus sixty is minus point-oh-one-two. It's been four years and five hundred and twelve days since the *Pennywise* saw it, which is four thousand five hundred and twelve days..."

"Sums," said Barnacle, "I want you to know that you are frightening me."

"...so we add a hundred and sixty-two, a hundred and eight, and minus fifty-four to the *Pennywise* position and we get..." She looked up. "Twenty-one eighty-two, sixteen, minus forty-nine."

"That's a guess," said Sorrow.

"It's a *prediction*," said Pemmy. "There's a difference. A guess is when you don't know what will happen. A prediction is when you say what will happen, and if it doesn't, you know you're wrong." She turned to the captain. "If we sail there and it isn't there, I'm wrong, and we've lost two days. If it is there..."

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. If it was there, every chart the Federation owned was wrong in a way nobody had suspected, and every plane on them might be somewhere else entirely.

Rhosyn looked at the scryer. Silver, empty, endless. Then she looked at the small gnome with ink on her chin.

"Sel," she said. "Lieutenant Ashgrove's heading, please."

"Heading zero-three-four, mark eleven," said the ship, with deep satisfaction. "I like her. Can we keep her?"

"Make it so."

The *Tranelis Selevarun* turned in the astral sea the way a thought turns in a mind: all at once, without effort, because he wanted to. The silver in the scryer shifted, steadied, and began to stream past.

"Captain's log, stardate 1.512.2, supplemental," Rhosyn said, not quite to anyone. "On the advice of my navigator, I am sailing toward a place where a plane is not, in the hope that it will be."

"I'd have phrased it with more confidence," said the ship.

"You'd have phrased it in verse."

"Well, *yes*."

---

She sent the report to Fleet that evening through the ship's standing channel to headquarters, a link that never closed, and listened to the clerks at Rosste receive it in polite silence. Then a voice she didn't know said, *Understood, Tranelis. Proceed. Keep us informed. And Captain... if your navigator is right, tell her not to tell anyone else until we've talked.*

"They're frightened," said Sel afterward, very quietly, when only she could hear.

"They should be," said Rhosyn. "So should we."

## III. The Thing on the Glass

Ondrevel was there.

It was where Pemmy had said it would be, give or take four leagues, which she would be apologizing for until the end of time. It hung in the astral sea like a coin in clear water: a great dark disc, its rim ragged with mountains, its face covered in forest so deep green it was nearly black. A small sun floated above it, pale as a pearl and cold-looking even from here, and under its light, here and there, stood towers. Thin, tall, stone towers, a hundred of them, perhaps more, and each one lit at the top by a lamp.

The barrier around it was invisible from outside. The ship could see straight through to the forest and the towers, as if Ondrevel were sitting under a glass bell. The owlin inside, Rhosyn had been told, would see only their god's stars.

The whole crew had come up to the bridge for the first sight of it, all twenty-six of them, crowding the rail around the scryer. Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Pemmy made a sound that was halfway between a laugh and a sob, and Tobin put his huge hand on her small shoulder, and the bridge broke out in cheering.

"*Four leagues*," Pemmy said, scrubbing at her eyes. "I was four leagues off. That's awful. That's appalling."

"That's four hundred leagues from where Fleet said it was," said Sorrow, and for the first time since leaving Rosste she sounded neither bored nor patient. She sounded shaken. "Pemberly. Do you understand what you've done?"

"I've found a plane that was where I said it'd be."

"You've found a plane that *moves*." Sorrow was looking past Ondrevel into the silver, where, a dozen leagues off, a second shape hung in the sea: a smooth dark sphere, unlit, featureless. "And so does that one. Sel, what's its position against the old chart?"

"Grandmother Lisk reported a dark companion sphere near Ondrevel," said the ship slowly. "It's moved the same distance. Same direction. The pair of them, side by side, like two boats in the same current."

"Like a whole *system* walking," said Pemmy.

Nobody said anything to that.

Barnacle broke the silence. He had climbed up onto the top of the scryer's cradle, which was strictly forbidden, and was crouched there with his wings half-open and his tail lashing.

"What," he said, "is *that*?"

They followed his gaze.

Something clung to the outside of Ondrevel's barrier, high above the forest. At first Rhosyn took it for a cloud. Then for a seed: a pale, round, ragged thing like a dandelion clock, the white puff of a gone-over flower, except that it was the size of a barn, and its filaments were spread flat against the invisible wall the way a hand is spread against a window.

It was not moving. It was pressed there, as if listening.

"Sorrow?" said Rhosyn.

The Chief Arcanist bent over her station, murmuring. A faint violet light rose in her eyes. "Magic," she said after a moment. "Faint. Not a kind I recognize. It's... reaching. Through the barrier. Not physically. It's like..." She frowned. "Like someone talking through a door."

"To whom?"

"No idea. Sel?"

"It's not a ship," said the ship. "It's not an organic, not one I've heard of. It's not swimming. It's not doing anything." He hesitated. "I don't like it. I'm not sure why. It's like seeing a man stand very still outside a house at night."

"Astral jellyfish," said Pemmy. "Maybe. There are things in the sea nobody's catalogued."

"It's not a jellyfish," said Barnacle. His fur was standing up all along his spine, and his voice had lost its usual drawl. "Jellyfish smell like jellyfish. I can't smell anything off that thing. Not at this distance, I know, but you know what I mean. It looks like it ought to smell. And I've got a feeling it doesn't."

Rhosyn did know what he meant. She had the same feeling, deep in the part of her mind she rarely opened. She did not open it now. The gift was hers alone, and it was tiring, and it came with a cost she had never quite been able to describe: a person's alignment arrived not as an idea but as a smell, honest and immediate and impossible to argue with, and once you had smelled someone's soul you could not easily forget it.

"We'll keep our distance from it," she said. "Sel, put us forty leagues off the barrier, on the far side of the disc from that... thing. Then we say hello."

## IV. The Golden Door

Master Tamsk Orrowin had kept the night watch on the Hollow Tower of Lammerlow for fifty-three years, and in all that time the sky had never changed.

That was the point of the sky. The Lantern-Keeper had written it once, at the beginning, and every star in it was a word, and the words never moved. A star-reader's task was not to discover. It was to read: to copy, to measure, to compare tonight's sky against the great charts in the tower's library and confirm, every night, that the god's writing was as it had always been. There were eleven thousand, four hundred and six stars in the sky over Ondrevel. Tamsk could name nine thousand of them.

His daughter Wennet had been able to name every one. And she had found one more.

He sat in the dome of the observatory with his feathers fluffed against the cold and the great brass sighting-tube swung toward the north, where, a year ago, before the fall, Wennet had pointed her own small tube and laughed, low, and said: *Papa. There's one we've missed.*

He had told her she was mistaken. They had argued about it for a week. It had been the last real argument of her life.

Now, every night, he sat here and waited for her to speak.

She had come back to him on the eighty-third night after the funeral, forty-one nights ago, as a voice. Not in his ears. Behind them. At first he had thought he was going mad, as old owlin sometimes do, and he had said nothing to anyone. But the voice had known things. It had known about the argument. It had known the words he'd said at the pyre, and the name of the sighting-tube she'd built at eleven. It said she was *beyond the stars*. That she was cold there. That she could see Ondrevel from outside, like a lamp in a window, and could not get in.

*There is a door, Papa,* the voice had said, three nights ago. *A door can be opened from inside. Only from inside. I'll teach you the words. When you see the door begin to open, you'll know I'm close.*

Tamsk was thinking of that when the new star appeared.

It came in the east, low, a single bright point where the god had written nothing: a hole in the sentence. His beak opened. He did not breathe. Fifty-three years he had read this sky, and it had never changed, and now...

The point of light stretched. It was a line, a burning golden line, taller than a tree, and he heard music, faint and sweet, a run of notes on some instrument he didn't know, like a harp and a voice at once. The line widened to a rectangle of light. The rectangle grew edges, carvings, ornaments, a lintel and posts of gold worked in leaves and waves and curling shells, and at last it stood in the meadow below the tower, three times the height of a man, as bright as a lamp, as ornate as a shrine: a tall golden door.

It swung open. Through it, Tamsk saw only plain grey light.

"*Wennet*," he said aloud.

He was halfway down the tower stairs before he knew he was moving. By the time he reached the meadow, half of Lammerlow was there, owlin in nightrobes and owlin in watch-cloaks, owlets clinging to their mothers' backs, all of them staring up with their great round eyes, and the Star-Warden herself pushing through the crowd with her staff.

Something came out of the door.

It was not Wennet. It was a ball of brass the size of a pumpkin, with four little arms and a lens on the front like a single unblinking eye, and it floated three feet off the ground with a soft whirring sound. It stopped. Its lens turned, taking in the crowd, the towers, the trees. Then it descended to the meadow and, with tremendous seriousness, began to dig.

The crowd watched in total silence as the brass ball scooped up a handful of black soil and put it in a little hatch in its side. It plucked a sprig of moonwort. It picked up a pebble, considered the pebble, put it back, and chose a better pebble.

Then it rose into the air, made a small polite whirring noise, and blinked out of existence. Gone. No door. Just gone.

The golden door swung shut, and the line of light shrank, and the point winked out, and the sky over Ondrevel was the same as it had always been.

Somewhere at the back of the crowd, an owlet began to cry.

Tamsk stood in the wet grass with his feathers flat against his body and his heart beating hard enough to hurt.

*Was that you?* he thought, as loudly as he could. *Was that the door? Wennet?*

For a long moment, nothing. Then, from behind his eyes, cold and very close:

*No, Papa. That was not my door. That was theirs. Be careful of them.*

---

On the far side of the barrier, the thing on the glass stirred for the first time.

Its filaments lifted from the invisible wall, one by one, and turned, like the heads of flowers following the sun, toward the place in the sea where a golden door had just closed.

## V. Questions

"It *moved*," said Sel. "The moment I opened the door. It moved toward us, Captain. I closed the door as fast as I could. I'm not proud of it. I slammed it like a boy hiding from his mother."

The ready room of the *Tranelis Selevarun* served also as council chamber and briefing room, which meant it had a long table, a short table, a map case, a window full of silver, and too few chairs. Rhosyn sat at the head of the long table. The others sat or stood: Tobin, Pemmy, Sorrow, and the two officers she had not yet heard from today. Lieutenant Brakka Thundergale, Security Chief, was a goliath, gray-skinned and stone-patterned, who had to duck under the lintel of every door on the ship and had stopped complaining about it after the third day. Lieutenant Hob Tumblewood, Chief Religionist, was a halfling of sixty, round and bald and tidy, who also ran the infirmary because the ship had only one cleric and someone had to.

Barnacle lay on the table in the middle of the briefing papers. Nobody had the heart to move him.

"Why would it move toward the door?" said Tobin.

"Because it was open," said Sorrow. She had a drawing in front of her, made from the scryer: the thing on the glass, its filaments spread. "Look at what it's doing. It's pressing on the barrier like it wants in and can't get in. Then a door opens. A hole. A way through the barrier."

"On my side," said Sel, "the door's just a plain opening in the drone bay. If that thing had reached me while the door was open, it could have gone in one end of my door and come out of the other, inside Ondrevel." A pause. "I *think*. I don't know if a barrier stops what comes through a portal. Nobody does. I'd rather not find out by experiment."

"Agreed," said Rhosyn. "No more doors while that's out there. Sorrow, the totem?"

"Done." The tiefling set it on the table: a disc of black soil fired hard with a sprig of moonwort pressed into it and a gray pebble at its center. "Ugly, but it'll hold. Shifting doesn't open a door. It's a jump, not a corridor. Nothing can follow you through a jump."

"So we shift," said Rhosyn. "Away team. Brakka, Hob, myself. We'll wear delegation earpieces."

Sorrow nodded slowly. "Good. Whatever that thing is, it's talking to someone down there. If it tries talking to you, you'll feel the shields get warm."

"Why the Chief Religionist?" said Tobin. "Not that you're not welcome, Hob."

"Because Sorrow says the thing is talking through the barrier like someone talking through a door," said Rhosyn. "And the people on the other side of that door believe their god wrote the stars. If something is talking to them from beyond the stars, they'll think it's holy. Or a ghost. I want someone with me who knows how to talk about gods and ghosts without lying."

Hob smiled, a little tiredly. "That's a short list. I'll go."

"Team Why," said Rhosyn. "Shifting at dawn, their time."

"Team *Why*," said Barnacle, from the middle of the papers. "Judge, I've been meaning to ask about that."

Tobin grinned. "She names the away teams after questions. Why, How, What, Where, When. Team Who is for when we need to find someone."

"What's Team Whether?"

"Diplomacy," said Rhosyn. "When we haven't decided yet."

The tressym considered this. "That's the most magistrate thing I've ever heard. I want to come."

"No."

"I'm an excellent judge of character."

"You're a cat."

"Exactly. Cats are the best judges of character in the multiverse. We've been judging you lot for thousands of years. We're just too polite to say."

"You've never been polite in your life," said Pemmy.

"I was polite once. I didn't care for it." Barnacle stood, stretched, and jumped down from the table. "Fine. I'll stay here and guard the Choirboy. Someone has to keep the rats in line, and frankly the Admiral is getting ideas."

When the others had gone, Rhosyn stayed in the ready room a little longer, looking out at the silver.

"Sel," she said.

"Captain."

"When you slammed the door. Did it hurt?"

A long pause. "No," said the ship. "But it was close. I could feel it coming. Like someone walking up behind you in an empty room. I didn't have a hull, before. I didn't know a hull could feel *watched*."

"You're doing well."

"I'm a bard, Captain. A dead one. I spent four hundred years singing about ships and I've been one for less than a year, and I just ran from a dandelion." His voice was rueful. "I'll do better."

"You did exactly right," said Rhosyn. "The first rule of any good court is that you don't let the defendant into the jury room."

"Is that a real rule?"

"It is now."

## VI. Lammerlow

They shifted down at dawn, which on Ondrevel was hardly brighter than night: the pearl-pale sun rising over the black forest like a lamp turned low.

Rhosyn felt the familiar lurch, the half-second of nowhere, and then wet grass under her boots and the smell of moonwort and cold stone. The meadow below the Hollow Tower was empty except for the dew. Then it wasn't: there were owlin in the trees, owlin on the tower stairs, owlin rising out of the long grass where they had been crouching, two dozen of them at least, with bows. They were taller than Rhosyn had expected, tall and narrow, with round faces of mottled brown and white feathers, and enormous dark eyes that did not blink.

"Easy," Rhosyn said quietly, to Brakka, whose hand had gone to her hammer. "Easy. Hands open."

She lifted her own hands, empty, and waited.

An old owlin came down the tower stairs: a female, barred gray and white, leaning on a staff carved from black wood. Her robe was embroidered with stars. She stopped ten paces off and studied the three of them for a long moment: the tall gray goliath, the round halfling, the human woman with the sword she had not drawn.

"You came out of the sky," the owlin said. Through the earpiece her words arrived as plain speech, a little dry, a little formal. "Last night a star appeared that the Lantern-Keeper never wrote. A door opened in it. A brass thing came out and stole a pebble. Then it vanished, and this morning you are standing in my meadow. I am Isk Fallowmere, Star-Warden of Lammerlow. I would like you to explain yourselves before my archers lose their patience. They have very little of it. Neither do I."

"I'm Captain Rhosyn Calder," said Rhosyn, "of the ship *Tranelis Selevarun*, of the Astral Federation. That door was ours. The brass thing was ours. The pebble, I'm afraid, was also ours; we needed it to come here without opening another door. I'm sorry we frightened your people. We came a long way to say hello, and we were late, because your world was not where we were told it would be."

The Star-Warden's head turned, slowly, almost all the way around, and then back. It was, Rhosyn would learn, the owlin equivalent of a raised eyebrow.

"Not where you were told," she repeated.

"It's a long story."

"Good," said Isk Fallowmere. "We are a people who like long stories. Come inside. Put away your weapons, Tall One."

"I haven't taken it out," said Brakka.

"Then keep not taking it out. That is the same thing as putting it away, for my purposes."

---

They talked for three hours in the long hall at the foot of the tower, over bowls of a hot bitter tea that tasted of bark and smoke. Rhosyn told the Star-Warden about the Federation, and Rosste, and the astral sea outside the stars, and the Star-Warden listened with her eyes half-lidded and asked short, precise, ruthless questions, and Rhosyn liked her immediately.

"You are telling me," said Isk at last, "that the sky is a wall. That the Lantern-Keeper's stars are written on the inside of it. That on the outside there is a silver sea full of ships and peoples and other worlds. That some of them have been watching us through the wall for years, and we never knew."

"Yes."

"And you expect me to believe this because a brass ball stole a pebble."

"No," said Rhosyn. "I expect you to believe it when you've tested it. I'd be disappointed in you if you believed it today." She set down her bowl. "We'd like to stay a while. Talk. Trade, if you want trade. We'll answer any question you ask. And when you're ready, if you ever are, we'll take some of your star-readers up to see the sea for themselves."

The Star-Warden was silent for a time. Then she said, "That is a better answer than I expected." She glanced, quick and sharp, toward the far end of the hall. "But before we speak of trade, Captain, there is something I must ask you. A star-reader of this tower has been hearing a voice. He says it is his dead daughter, speaking to him from beyond the stars. Half my town thinks he is mad. The other half thinks he is blessed. And now you come and tell me there *is* a beyond the stars." Her great eyes fixed on Rhosyn. "So I must ask: is it you? Is it your people who speak to him?"

Rhosyn felt Hob go very still beside her.

"No," she said. "But I think I've seen what does."

---

Master Tamsk Orrowin was waiting for them at the top of the Hollow Tower, in the cold dome of the observatory, and he was not pleased to see them.

He was old even by owlin reckoning, his feathers gone dull and ragged at the edges, and he sat in a chair beside the great brass sighting-tube with a ledger on his knees and his talons curled tight around a pen. The dome above him was open to the sky. The sky was dark and full of fixed stars.

Floating at his shoulder, without oil or wick or anything else to feed it, was a small cold flame, the color of ice.

"She told me to be careful of you," he said, before anyone had spoken.

"Who did?" said Rhosyn.

"My daughter."

"When?"

"Last night. After your door." The old owlin's head turned slowly toward Hob. "That one is a priest. I can see it on him. What is he here for? To tell me I'm mad?"

"No," said Hob gently. "I'm here because the captain thought someone should be here who'd lost people. I've buried a great many. I don't think you're mad, Master Orrowin. I think you're grieving, and I think something is speaking to you. Those can both be true."

The owlin stared at him. Some of the stiffness went out of his shoulders.

"May I sit?" said Rhosyn. "I'd like to hear about her. Your daughter. From the beginning, if you'll tell it."

He told it.

Wennet Orrowin had been the finest star-reader Lammerlow had produced in a century. She had memorized the whole sky by the age of nine. She had built her own sighting-tube at eleven and named it Stubborn, because it was. At twenty-six she had come to her father and told him that she had found a star nobody had ever catalogued: faint, far north, just under the constellation of the Heron's Eye. He had told her she was mistaken. The sky was written; it was complete; there were eleven thousand four hundred and six stars and no more. She had said she would prove it. She had gone up the north tower to take another measurement, at night, in winter, alone, and the ice on the stair had...

He stopped there. Nobody asked him to go on.

"And then she came back," he said after a while. "Eighty-three nights after. As a voice. I didn't tell anyone, at first. I thought I was going the way old star-readers sometimes go. But she knew things. She knew about the argument. She knew Stubborn's name. She knew what I said at the pyre."

"What else did she say?"

"That she's cold. That she's outside the sky, beyond the stars, and she can see Ondrevel from out there, like a lamp in a window, and she can't get in." His talons tightened on the pen. "That there's a door. That a door can be opened from inside, only from inside, and she'd teach me the words, and when the door was ready she'd come home."

"Did she teach you the words?" said Rhosyn.

"Yes."

"And she gave you that?" Rhosyn nodded at the little cold flame.

The old owlin looked at it, almost fondly. "A gift. To light my work, so I wouldn't need oil. She said there would be other gifts, when she was home."

Hob drew a slow breath through his nose and let it out again.

"Master Orrowin," Rhosyn said. "You're a star-reader. You keep records."

"Of course."

"Of the voice? Of each night it spoke?"

He hesitated. Then he held out the ledger.

It was beautiful. She had expected nothing less from a man who had spent fifty-three years measuring things that never changed. Each night had its own entry, timed to the minute by the transit of a named star; each entry gave what the voice had said, as close to word for word as he could recall, and his own remarks underneath in a smaller hand. Rhosyn turned the pages slowly. She had read a great many depositions in her life. She read this one the same way.

Night one: *She said, Papa, it's me. She said, I'm cold.* Underneath, in the small hand: *Not possible. Grief. Wrote of Stubborn last night in my private book to steady myself; perhaps I dream of it.*

Night four: *She spoke of Stubborn. Knew its name.*

Night nine: *She spoke of our argument. Said she forgave me.*

Night twelve: *I asked her: what is the name of the star you found? She said: You'll know when you see me, Papa.* Underneath: *She was always one for surprises.*

Night thirty: *She told me what I said at the pyre.* Underneath: *I have not told this to anyone. Not even Isk.*

Night thirty-eight: *Words for the door. Learned the first part.*

Rhosyn turned back to night twelve and read it again.

She felt it then, faintly: a warmth at her ear, where the earpiece sat. Not hot. Just warm, the way a cup is warm a minute after the tea's been poured out. Beside her, Brakka shifted and put a hand up to her own ear, frowning.

Tamsk had gone still, his head tilted, listening to something only he could hear.

"She's here," he said softly. "She says... she says you should not read that. That it's private."

"Of course," said Rhosyn, and closed the ledger, and handed it back. "Forgive me."

The warmth at her ear faded.

She looked at Hob. Hob looked at her. Neither of them said anything.

"There's one more thing I'd like to see," said Rhosyn, "if you'll let me. Her room. Her work. Whatever she left."

---

Wennet Orrowin's study was at the foot of the north tower, small, cold, and painfully tidy. The tidiness was her father's, Rhosyn suspected: a year's worth of dusting. Wennet's own mess survived only in the margins of her charts, where her handwriting raced and looped and argued with itself.

On the desk, squared precisely to the edge, was a single folded letter, sealed with blue wax. On the outside, in that same racing hand: *For Papa. Open on the night I make master.*

"She left it on her desk the week before she fell," said Tamsk. "I never opened it." He touched the seal with one talon, very lightly. "She hadn't made master yet. It wasn't mine to open."

"No," Rhosyn agreed. "It wasn't."

Rhosyn opened the part of her mind she rarely opened.

She did it carefully, the way you open a door onto a cold night. It took effort, as it always did, a kind of leaning inward. Then the room arrived, as smell: the dusty, honest, ordinary smell of an old study, ink and cold stone, and over it, Tamsk.

He smelled of chalk and cold tea and grief. Underneath that, warm and steady, the clean, plain, almost bread-like smell that she had learned over twenty years to recognize as good. A careful man. A kind one.

But there was something else. A thread of it, thin as a hair. Not his own: it didn't sit in him the way the rest did. It ran out of him, like a cord tied around his wrist and leading somewhere else. It smelled like a candle the moment after it's been pinched out.

She closed the door in her mind and found she was holding the edge of the desk.

"Captain?" said Hob quietly.

"I'm fine." She wasn't, entirely. "Master Orrowin, thank you. I'd like to speak with my people for a moment."

## VII. The Boarding

The thing was fast.

That was the first thing Sel learned about it, and it was the most important, and he learned it too late. It had hung on the glass of Ondrevel's barrier for all those hours as still as a painting, and he had come to think of it as slow, the way you think a heron is slow until it strikes. Then, at the eleventh hour of the away team's visit, it let go of the barrier and came at him across forty leagues of silver in less time than it took him to say *Commander*.

"*Commander!*" said Sel.

Tobin Marrow was on the bridge alone, in the captain's chair, reading Pemmy's notes on drift with his lips moving. He looked up. He saw the scryer. He was out of the chair before Sel had finished the word.

"All hands, brace. Sorrow to the bridge. Team How to the drone bay. Sel, can you outrun it?"

"I'm trying!"

He *was* trying. He wanted, with everything he had, to be somewhere else, and in the astral sea wanting is moving, and the *Tranelis Selevarun* leapt away across the silver like a deer. But the thing was faster. It billowed out behind him, its filaments spreading into a white net a quarter of a league across, and it closed on him the way a hand closes on a moth.

It hit him amidships.

Sel had been a ship for three hundred and eleven days. He had felt rain on his hull and the warmth of the crew moving inside him like blood. He had never been hurt.

It was like being stung by a hundred bees at once, all down one side. He felt the filaments go in. Not through the hull, not quite, but *into* it, thin and white and searching, finding seams and hatches and the joins between planks, pushing. One found the drone bay door. One found the grove. One found the small round porthole of the tavern and pressed against the glass.

And through all of them, whispering.

*Let me in.*

"It's *in my hull*," said Sel, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "Tobin, it's in my hull, it's in the grove, it's..."

"I know, lad. We've got you. Team How!"

Down in the drone bay, Crewmate Odo Penhallow, a crewdwarf with a spear and an enormous beard, lowered his weapon and began, quietly, to weep.

"Odo?" said Ensign Faye Lark, beside him.

"It's my mam," said the dwarf. "She's saying... she's *calling* me..."

All over the ship it was the same. The filaments were in the walls, and the whisper was in the filaments, and the whisper had a hundred voices. Each one was someone dead. A mother, a husband, a child, an old friend. *Let me in. Let me in. I'm cold out here. Open the door.*

On the bridge, Pemmy came in at a run and stopped dead.

"Gran?" she said.

Sorrow Vey, half a step behind her, grabbed the gnome by the collar and hauled her back. The tiefling's face was gray, and her tail was rigid. "It's not your gran. It's not mine. I don't *have* one. Pemberly, listen to me, it's in your head with *words*." She swung toward the ceiling. "Sel! It's using words. Give them something louder!"

For one stunned moment the ship didn't understand.

Then he did.

He had been a bard for four hundred years. Long before he'd known how to be a ship, he had known what music does to a frightened room.

The *Tranelis Selevarun* began to play.

It burst out of every wall at once: *Orlalassar's Battle Theme*, the one with the drums and the brass and the rising horns, written two centuries ago for an elven war fleet that had broken a siege. Sel played it the way he had played it in taverns on the eve of battles, too loud and too fast and with every voice he had. The whisper did not stop. But it was drowned, suddenly, under a wall of sound that said *stand up* and *hold the line* and *not today*.

In the drone bay, Odo Penhallow wiped his face with the back of his fist, picked up his spear, and drove it into the nearest white filament with a roar.

"It's *not my mam!*" he bellowed. "My mam was *horrible!*"

The filament split like a reed. Something like milk ran out of it.

"Ha!" shouted Faye Lark. "They break! They break easy!"

All over the ship, Team How and everyone else went at the white threads with spears and knives and kitchen cleavers. Sorrow Vey stood on the bridge with her hands raised and fire running down her fingers, and the scryer showed the thing outside burning in long violet streaks wherever her bolts struck it.

In the druid grove, Barnacle found the heart of it.

The grove was at the stern, a long room under a glass roof, full of small trees and herbs in beds of real earth. The thickest filaments had come in here, through the seams of the glass, and hung down among the branches like gray moss, and where they touched the leaves the leaves were wilting. At the center of the tangle, half through the roof, was a lump. A seed. A hard, round, pale thing like a nut the size of a man's head, with the filaments growing out of it in every direction.

Barnacle crouched in the herb bed and looked at it. His ears went flat. His tail went thick.

He had been hunting things in dark places all his life. Rats in grain-holds, mice in sailcloth, the Admiral in the walls of this very ship. Every one of them had a smell. Fear, mostly. Hunger. Life. Even dead things had a smell. It was the first thing he knew about anything.

This thing smelled of nothing at all. Not alive. Not dead. Nothing. Like a drawing of a fish.

"Oh, I don't *like* you," Barnacle said.

He jumped.

He hit the seed claws-first, all four feet, and his wings beat hard to keep him on it while the filaments thrashed. He bit. His teeth skidded on the shell, found a seam, went in. Something inside the seed went *crack*.

The whole thing *shrieked*.

Not with sound. With everything else. Every whisper on the ship turned into a scream at once, and Sel felt the filaments convulse inside his hull, and then they were pulling *out*, ripping out through the seams and hatches, and the thing tore loose from him with a wrench he felt from prow to stern. Barnacle was flung into the lemon tree.

"*It's going!*" said Sel. "It's going, it's running, it's..."

The scryer showed it: the white puff, burned and torn and half its size, streaming away across the silver toward Ondrevel's barrier, trailing broken threads.

"Let it go," said Tobin. "Sel. *Let it go.* Damage report."

The music stopped. In the sudden quiet, everyone could hear the ship breathing, which ships do not do.

"I'm..." said Sel. "Seams sprung in the grove. Drone bay door's scratched. Tavern porthole cracked. Nothing that won't mend." A pause. "Odo says his mam was horrible."

"I heard," said Tobin, and sat down heavily in the captain's chair. "Sorrow?"

The tiefling was crouched on the bridge floor beside a fragment that had fallen in through a hatch: a piece of pale shell like a broken pot, as big as a dinner plate, still steaming faintly where her fire had caught it. She turned it over.

On the inside, burned into the shell, was a mark. A twisting, ugly, angular sign, like a spider made of hooks.

"Get me the captain," said Sorrow.

In the grove, Barnacle climbed down out of the lemon tree, shook himself, and sneezed.

"Choirboy," he said, to the ceiling. "Was that a good song or a bad song? I couldn't tell. I was busy."

"It was the best song I've ever played," said Sel shakily.

"Good. Play it at my funeral. Not soon." Barnacle limped over to where a broken filament lay among the herbs and sniffed it, carefully. Then he sat back. "Nothing," he said. "Still nothing. Tell the Judge, will you. It smells of absolutely nothing. I want that on the record."

## VIII. They're Hurting Her

In the study at the foot of the north tower, Tamsk Orrowin screamed.

It was a terrible sound: high and raw, not like an owl at all. He clutched his head with both hands. The cold flame at his shoulder flared white.

"*They're hurting her!*"

"Master Orrowin..."

"Your *ship!*" He wheeled on Rhosyn, feathers bristling, eyes enormous. "She says your ship is hurting her! She says it's *killing* her! She's screaming, she's... *Wennet!*"

At the same moment, the earpiece at Rhosyn's ear went hot, so hot she gasped. Brakka swore and clapped a hand to her own ear. And under the heat, very small and very far away, a voice: Tobin's, through the ship's channel, tight and fast.

"*...Captain, we've been attacked. The thing from the barrier came at us and tried to come aboard. We drove it off. It's wounded. It's running back toward Ondrevel. No one's dead. Sel's hurt but he says he'll mend. Captain, respond...*"

Rhosyn didn't respond. She was looking at Tamsk.

He was standing in the middle of his daughter's study with his head in his talons, screaming that a ship was hurting her, at the precise moment that her ship had, in fact, hurt something.

"Hob," she said quietly.

"I heard it," said the halfling. His round face was very pale.

"Master Orrowin," said Rhosyn. "Listen to me. What's hurting her is the same thing..."

"You *lied*," said Tamsk. "She told me. She *told* me to be careful of you." He flung out one talon, and the cold flame at his shoulder leapt from it like a spark from a fire: a bolt of ice-blue light that cracked against the wall beside Brakka's head and left the stone frosted white. Brakka threw up her arm. When she lowered it, the doorway was empty, and Tamsk's footsteps were going up the north tower stairs, fast, uneven, the steps of an old man running on ice.

"Captain." Brakka had her hammer out. "Permission to go after him."

"Wait."

"*Captain...*"

"Wait." Rhosyn pressed the earpiece. "Tobin. Is anyone hurt?"

"*Bruised, mostly. Odo cried, then hit things. Sel's sore. Barnacle says the thing smells of nothing, and he wants that on the record.*"

"It's on the record. Tobin, listen. When the thing attacked you, did it say anything?"

A pause. "*It whispered. Everybody heard someone dead. Their own dead. Asking to be let in. Captain, Sorrow's found something in the wreckage. A mark burned inside its shell. She wants to talk to you.*"

"Put her on."

"*Captain.*" Sorrow's voice, quick and tight. "*There's a sign burned inside the creature. Like a spider made of hooks. I've never seen it. It's not any school I know. It looks like a sigil. A signature.*"

Rhosyn turned to Hob. "The voice taught him words. For a door. Words go with a circle, usually. With signs."

"Usually," said Hob. "Captain, he'll have drawn it. Somewhere high. Where he can see the sky."

"North tower roof," said Brakka. "That's where those footsteps are going."

"Captain," said Sorrow, through the earpiece. "*What's going on down there?*"

Rhosyn was already moving. "Someone's about to open a door," she said. "And I'd like to know who's on the other side before he does. Brakka, Hob, with me. Nobody hits anybody."

"Captain..."

"*Nobody hits anybody*, Lieutenant. If you knock him down, he'll get up again tomorrow, and the voice will still be there. I want him to stop. Not to be stopped."

## IX. The Dark Door

The roof of the north tower was a flat stone platform ringed by a waist-high wall, open to the sky. Ice glittered on the flagstones. A chalk circle had been drawn in the middle of it, wide enough for three men to lie down in, filled with signs; and at the center of the signs, larger than the rest, was a twisting, ugly, angular mark like a spider made of hooks.

Tamsk Orrowin stood inside the circle with his arms raised to the stars, chanting.

Rhosyn didn't know the language. The earpiece should have made it plain to her; it made anything spoken plain. This it gave her only in pieces, as if the words were too sharp to carry whole. A name, over and over: *Ozhkavarr. Ozhkavarr.* And around the name, words that seemed to mean *open* and *come* and *I make the way*.

Above the circle, the air was going dark.

It began as a point of black: a hole in the night, darker than the night around it. Then the point stretched. It became a line, a crooked black line, taller than the tower. The line widened into a narrow rectangle, ragged at the edges like torn cloth. And it grew a frame. Not gold. Black iron, rough and crusted, worked in hooks and teeth and things that were not quite faces.

It was a door. A mockery of Sel's door, as if someone had heard a description of it and hated it.

It did not open. Not yet. But the surface of it bulged, slowly, as if something on the other side were leaning on it.

"*Captain,*" said Sel, in her ear, very quietly. "*I can see that from here. Through the barrier. It's... Captain, what is that?*"

"I don't know yet."

She opened the door in her mind.

She did it fast this time, without care, and the smell came in like a blow.

Rot. Rot and burnt sugar and old blood, a slaughterhouse in high summer with the doors shut. It was chaos and it was cruelty and it was *hunger*, endless, mindless, enormous, pressing against the black door from the other side. She had smelled murderers. She had smelled a man who had drowned his own children. She had never smelled anything like this. It was so much worse than evil that the word stopped meaning anything.

She staggered. Brakka caught her elbow.

"Captain!"

"I'm all right." She wasn't. She made herself keep the door in her mind open a moment longer and turned her attention, like turning her head, toward Tamsk. Chalk, tea, grief. Good, frightened, small. And the thin thread of pinched-out candle smoke that ran out of him, out and up, toward the stars, toward the barrier, toward the place where something wounded clung to the outside of the world.

That thread smelled of *nothing*. She followed it as far as she could, and at the end of it there was no one. No soul. Not even a bad one.

*A drawing of a fish.*

She closed her mind and opened her eyes.

"Tobin," she said. "Sorrow. The thing on the barrier doesn't smell. Barnacle's right. There's nothing in it. Nobody home." She took a breath. "The thing behind that door smells like every bad thing I've ever smelled, all at once. They're not the same creature. But they're tied together. The one with no smell is the one that's been talking to him. And its sigil is in the middle of this circle."

"*A puppet*," said Sorrow, slowly. "*Something made it. Something put its sign in it and threw it out into the sea and...*" Her voice changed. "*Captain, if Barnacle hadn't cracked that seed, it would have come aboard. It would have whispered to us all. Every one of us.*"

"I know." Rhosyn was looking at the door. "Sorrow, can we break the circle?"

"*Not mid-summoning. Not safely. Every arcanist learns that in the first year. A circle half-finished is worse than either. Things get loose. Things get halfway through.*"

"Then he has to stop it himself."

"Captain," said Brakka, low and urgent, "I can reach him in two strides."

"And he'll draw it again tomorrow. And the voice will teach him again. And next time we won't be here." Rhosyn stepped up to the edge of the chalk and knelt on the ice. "Master Orrowin."

He didn't look at her. He went on chanting. *Ozhkavarr. Ozhkavarr.* The door bulged.

"Master Orrowin, you're a star-reader. You measure twice."

The chanting faltered for half a word.

"You told me so yourself," Rhosyn went on, steadily. "Fifty-three years of reading a sky that never changes. You don't believe a thing because you want it to be true. You check. Your daughter came to you with a star nobody had seen, and you didn't believe her. Not because you didn't love her. Because she hadn't proved it yet."

"*Be quiet.*"

"I read your ledger. Night four, she knew Stubborn's name. You'd written it down four nights before, in your private book, to steady yourself. Night nine, she spoke of the argument. Night thirty, she told you what you said at the pyre. The words you'd never told anyone." Rhosyn kept her voice very calm. "But you knew them, Master Orrowin. Every single thing she told you, you already knew. Forty-one nights, and she never told you one thing that wasn't already in your own head."

The door bulged. Somewhere behind it, something made a noise like an enormous animal shifting its weight.

"Night twelve," Rhosyn said. "You asked her the name of her star. The one thing *she* knew and you didn't. And she said: *you'll know when you see me, Papa.* In my old court, Master Orrowin, that's what a witness says when they don't know the answer."

The old owlin's arms were shaking.

"She *knew* things," he whispered. "She knew..."

"Your earpiece," Rhosyn said, "the warm one, at my ear... it gets warm when someone tries to get into my head. It went warm in her study, when your daughter told you I shouldn't read your ledger. It's hot now." It was. It was burning, a hard bright pain under her jaw. Brakka was gritting her teeth. "Something is trying very hard to get into my head right now, Master Orrowin. Same as it got into yours. That's how it knows things. It reads them. It reads you, and it gives you back what it finds."

"*Liar,*" said Tamsk, but it was barely a breath.

"Then test me." Rhosyn turned her head. "Hob."

The halfling came forward. In his hands, held carefully, flat, the way you'd hold a sleeping bird, was a folded letter with a blue wax seal.

Tamsk made a sound.

"I took it from the desk," Hob said softly. "I'm sorry. I thought you'd want it close. And I thought..." He swallowed. "I thought you might want to know."

"There's one thing in this world your daughter knew and you don't," said Rhosyn. "It's in there. You've never read it. You've thought about what it says, I expect, every night for a year. But you've never read it. So ask her. Ask her what it says. Then open it."

"Captain," said Brakka, very quietly, "if she gets it right..."

"Then I'll stand aside," said Rhosyn, "and he can finish his words. And whatever comes through that door, Lieutenant, we'll deal with it." She didn't look away from Tamsk. "That's the offer. A fair test. If she's your daughter, she'll pass it. Ask her."

For a long moment the old owlin stood frozen, with his arms half-raised and the black door bulging above him. Then, slowly, he lowered his arms.

"Wennet," he said aloud. "Wennet, my heart. What did you write in your letter?"

His head tilted. Listening.

Rhosyn's earpiece blazed. Somewhere at the edge of hearing she caught it, faint, the voice that wasn't hers to hear: cold and close and in a hurry.

"She says," said Tamsk, and his voice shook, "she says it says *I love you, Papa*. She says it says she's sorry. For the argument. That she was wrong about the star, and she's sorry, and she loves me." He was weeping now. "She says *finish the words, Papa. Finish the words. They're coming to hurt me again.*"

Hob held out the letter.

Tamsk took it. His talons were shaking so badly that it took him three tries to break the seal. He unfolded it. And Rhosyn, who could see it upside down in the starlight, saw that it was very short.

He read it aloud. He didn't seem to choose to. It came out of him the way breath does.

"*Papa. Measured eleven times, from three towers, over four hundred nights. It's real. North, just under the Heron's Eye, a hair to the left. Eleven thousand four hundred and seven. I've named it Patience, after the only thing you ever had too much of. Now stop checking my sums. W.*"

The roof was silent.

"She wasn't sorry," Tamsk said. His voice had gone very small and very strange. "She was *right*. She'd never have said sorry, she was *right*, and she *knew* she was right, and she..." He made a sound that was half laugh and half sob. "*Patience*. She named it *Patience*. She was laughing at me."

"*Finish the words,*" hissed something cold, and this time Rhosyn heard it plainly, not through Tamsk but past him, through the burning earpiece, in a voice that had stopped bothering to sound like anyone. "*Finish the words. Finish the words. Open the door. OPEN THE...*"

"It told me what I wanted," said Tamsk. He was looking at the letter. "Every night. It told me exactly what I wanted to hear."

He stepped to the edge of the circle, and with one foot, carefully, the way an old star-reader corrects a mistake in a chart, he scuffed out the hooked spider at its center.

The black door *screamed*.

For an instant, as the iron frame bent and tore, it swung open the width of a hand, and through that gap Rhosyn saw *down*. A drop with no bottom. Layers, like broken plates stacked in the dark, one below the next below the next, more than she could count, and on every one of them things moving, crawling, climbing over one another, an endless seething press of them. Close to the gap, closer than anything, one shape stood out: something huge dragging something huger behind it, a great black hook of iron, wider than a house, on a chain that ran back into the dark. And the smell came out of it in a wave, rot and sugar and blood, so strong that Hob fell to his knees.

Then the iron frame folded in on itself, like paper in a fire, and the door shrank to a line, and the line to a point, and the point to nothing.

"*NOW,*" said Sel, in her ear.

## X. Nothing

He had been following it.

Sel had not let it go, not really, whatever Tobin said. He had trailed after it across the forty leagues at a polite and careful distance, the way a sea-elf boy trails a wounded shark: close enough to watch, far enough to run. He had watched it crawl back onto Ondrevel's barrier, torn and burning and half its old size, and spread its ruined filaments against the glass, and *push*. He had felt it push, even from where he was. He had seen the black door begin to form on the tower roof below it.

And he had listened, through the captain's earpiece, to everything.

When Tamsk scuffed out the sigil, the thing on the barrier convulsed. All its filaments went rigid at once. It peeled half off the glass, flailing, blind, like a spider whose web has been cut, and for one long moment it was paying no attention whatsoever to the sea behind it.

"*NOW*," said Sel, and on the bridge Sorrow Vey raised both hands and Tobin Marrow said "*Ram it, lad,*" and the *Tranelis Selevarun* came out of the silver with *Orlalassar's Battle Theme* roaring from every wall, the final movement, the one where the horns go up and up and don't come down.

He hit it prow-first.

It burst. There was no other word for it. It burst like a dandelion clock in a child's breath, into ten thousand white threads that spun away across the silver and crumbled, as they spun, into dust, and the dust drifted, and was gone.

The music stopped.

In the observatory dome of the Hollow Tower, Tamsk Orrowin's cold flame went out.

Sel hung in the sea beside Ondrevel's barrier, breathing, which ships do not do.

"Sorrow," he said, after a while. "When things die. Things with... with people in them. What happens? Do you see anything? Feel anything?"

"Sometimes," said Sorrow slowly. "If you're looking for it. A kind of... letting go."

"Did you feel anything just now?"

The tiefling was quiet for a long moment.

"No," she said. "Nothing. It was empty, Sel. There was nobody in it at all."

"Good," said the ship. And then, much more quietly: "I didn't want to have killed someone. Not on my first..."

"You didn't," said Tobin. "You broke a thing. A thing someone made and threw at us. That's all." He was quiet for a moment too. "Well played, lad."

"Thank you, Commander."

On the scryer, Ondrevel turned slowly in the silver: the black forest, the pale sun, the hundred lamp-lit towers. The glass around it was clean.

## XI. Patience

They sat on the roof of the north tower until the pale sun was high, because Tamsk would not go down, and Rhosyn would not leave him.

The chalk circle was smeared to nothing. Brakka had seen to that, very thoroughly, with her boot and then with a bucket of water, and then, when the water froze, with her hammer. Nobody had stopped her. Afterward she had gone to stand at the wall with her back to them, keeping watch on a sky that no longer needed watching, which Rhosyn understood was the goliath's way of giving an old man some privacy.

Tamsk sat with his back against the wall and the letter in his lap.

"I should have known," he said at last. "Forty-one nights. I'm a star-reader. I should have *known*."

"You did know," said Rhosyn. "Night twelve. You wrote it down. *She was always one for surprises.* You noticed it didn't answer. You just didn't want to have noticed." She sat down beside him on the cold stone. "That's not foolishness, Master Orrowin. That's what love does to evidence. I've seen it a hundred times. The finest witnesses I ever heard couldn't see the thing in front of them when it was someone they loved."

He was quiet for a long time. Then he turned his great round eyes on Hob, who was sitting on his other side with his hands folded over his belly.

"Priest," he said. "Where is she?"

Hob didn't answer right away. Rhosyn had seen him do this before, with the dying and with their families: take a question seriously enough to be slow about it.

"I don't know," he said at last. "Nobody does. Not really. My people say one thing, yours say another, and the Federation's full of people who say a dozen things more. I've buried a great many and I've never once had one of them come back and tell me." He looked out at the sky. "But I'll tell you what I do know. Wherever she is, she isn't in that thing. That thing didn't know her. It never knew her. It only knew you." He nodded at the letter. "*That's* her. That's the only piece of her that's talked to you this whole year. And she was laughing at you, and she loved you, and she was right."

Tamsk looked down at the letter for a long time.

"Eleven thousand four hundred and seven," he said. "Under the Heron's Eye. A hair to the left." He lifted his head. "I'll measure it tonight. And tomorrow. And the night after. Eleven times, from three towers. I'll check her sums." His beak clicked, softly; it might have been a laugh. "She'd hate that."

"She'd expect it," said Rhosyn.

"Yes." He folded the letter, very carefully, along its old folds. "Yes, she would."

Rhosyn hesitated. Then she reached up and unhooked the earpiece from her own ear.

"My ship," she said, "was a bard, before he was a ship. He'd like to play something for her, if you'll let him. He can't come down. But you can hear him through this."

The old owlin looked at the little brass hook for a long moment. Then he held out his talon.

She put the earpiece into it, and he raised it to the side of his head, under the feathers, where an owl's ears are, and closed his eyes.

Rhosyn couldn't hear what Sel played. She didn't need to. She knew which piece it would be: *Oribella's Theme*, the slow one, the one that sounds like a love song until the last bars, when you realize it has been a lament the whole time. She watched it reach the old star-reader's face. She watched him listen to it all the way through, his eyes closed and his feathers lifting slightly in the cold wind.

When it was done, he gave her back the earpiece and said, "Thank your ship."

"He heard you," said Rhosyn.

---

The Star-Warden met them at the foot of the tower.

"I watched from my own roof," said Isk Fallowmere. "I saw your door last night, Captain, and I saw that other door this morning. I know which I would rather have in my sky." She looked up at the north tower, where a small gray-white figure was already swinging a brass sighting-tube toward the north. "What was it? The thing that spoke to him?"

"I don't know," said Rhosyn. She had decided, sometime in the last hour, that she would say that as many times as she needed to and never once pretend otherwise. "I know it was outside your sky, pressing on it. I know it couldn't get in by itself; it needed someone inside to open the way. I know it had no soul in it that anyone could find, and I know it was tied to something on the other side of that black door that had a great deal of soul, all of it rotten. I know it read your star-reader's mind and fed him back his own hopes. That's what I know." She paused. "What I don't know is what it was, who made it, or whether there are more of them."

The Star-Warden absorbed this in silence.

"That," she said, "is the most honest thing anyone has said to me in forty years of being Star-Warden." Her head turned slowly, nearly all the way around, and back. "Come back tomorrow, Captain. Bring your arcanist. And your navigator, the one who found us when we weren't where we should be. I find I have a great many questions, and very little patience." The owlin's beak clicked. "We have, it seems, given all our patience to a star."

## XII. Tavern Rules

That night the *Tranelis Selevarun* held a party in the tavern, because a ship that has been boarded and survived should, in Tobin's professional opinion, drink about it.

The tavern porthole had a crack across it. Sel insisted it gave the place character. Odo Penhallow had been bought eleven drinks by eleven crewmates on the strength of his war cry, which had already become a saying aboard ship: whenever anyone was nervous, someone else would say *it's not my mam, my mam was horrible*, and everybody would laugh. Faye Lark was teaching the crewdwarf to dance. Sorrow sat in a corner with the piece of shell on the table in front of her, turning it over and over, frowning at the hooked spider burned inside it, a glass of something purple untouched by her elbow.

Pemmy had climbed up on a table and was explaining drift to anyone who would listen, with diagrams, in spilled ale.

"...because if *one* plane walks, why wouldn't they all? Why would Ondrevel be special? If it's not special, then every plane on every chart has been walking since the day it was charted, and nobody noticed because we only started writing things down five hundred days ago..."

"Sums," said Barnacle from the rafters, "you're getting ale on my favorite beam."

"Sorry, Barnacle."

"And you're frightening the junior officers."

"I'm frightening *Fleet*," said Pemmy, with a certain grim pride, and sat down. "They sent a *sending* back. Fifth-mag, an hour's conference with three admirals. They want my sums, both sightings, every number. And they want me to tell nobody else."

"Oh, well done," said Barnacle. "You've told the whole tavern."

"The tavern doesn't count. The tavern's family."

"I'll allow it," said Barnacle generously.

Around the eleventh hour, inevitably, someone shouted for a song, and the ship obliged. He sang a shanty of the sea-elf fleets, all about a captain and a mermaid and a very long anchor-chain, and it was in the second verse that it became clear what *sort* of shanty it was. Sel sang it with tremendous gusto. The crew roared along.

Rhosyn appeared in the tavern doorway halfway through the third verse.

The singing faltered. Sel's voice did not.

"*...and she said to the captain, 'that's a fine bit of chain, but I've seen bigger...*'"

"Sel," said the captain.

"Captain! We're in the tavern."

Rhosyn considered this.

"We are," she agreed, and came in, and took the stool beside Tobin, and accepted a mug of ale. "Carry on."

The tavern cheered. The ship carried on. Rhosyn did not sing along, but Tobin noted, for the record, that her foot was tapping.

## XIII. Captain's Log

"Captain's log, stardate 1.515."

The bridge was empty again except for her and the scryer, and the scryer was full of Ondrevel: the black forest, the pale sun, and on one tall tower in the north, a single tiny lamp.

"We've spent three days at Ondrevel. First contact has been established with the owlin of Lammerlow through their Star-Warden, Isk Fallowmere, who has agreed to further talks. She has asked that our next visit include Lieutenant Commander Vey and Lieutenant Ashgrove, and has requested, in writing, that we not open any more golden doors in her sky without warning her first. I've agreed. Ship has agreed. Ship has asked me to add that it was a very *good* door."

"It was," said Sel. "It had shells on."

"On stardate 1.514 the *Tranelis Selevarun* was attacked and briefly boarded by an unknown creature resembling a giant seed or dandelion head, which had been clinging to the outside of Ondrevel's barrier. The creature communicated by telepathy, imitating the voices of the dead. The same creature, or something speaking through it, had for forty-one nights been in contact with an owlin star-reader, Master Tamsk Orrowin, posing as his dead daughter, and had taught him a summoning. Master Orrowin, at the last moment, declined to complete it. The creature was destroyed. The summoning was not completed. I want both of those sentences in that order."

She paused, thinking.

"Observations, for Fleet. One. The creature appears to have been unable to cross the barrier on its own. It could reach through with its mind, but not with its body, and its whole effort was to have someone inside open a door for it, or for whatever it served. Two. Ship's portal attracted it. We don't know whether a portal could carry such a thing through a barrier. Until we do, I recommend no ship open a portal into a plane with one of these things nearby. Three. It had no soul that anyone could detect. Not a dead one, not an evil one. Nothing. Four. The thing behind the black door had a great deal of soul, and I am not going to describe the smell of it in a log. Five. There was a mark inside the creature's shell that matched the central sign of the summoning circle. Lieutenant Commander Vey believes the creature was made, and sent, by whatever was waiting behind that door. I agree. Six." She hesitated. "Six. Through the door, I saw something being dragged. A great iron hook on a chain. I don't know what it was. I don't know what it was for. I didn't like it."

"Noted," said Sel quietly.

"Seven, and most important. If one of these things found Ondrevel by chance, out of the whole astral sea, then either Ondrevel was very unlucky or there are a great many of them. I don't believe in luck. Recommend all ships keep watch for anything pressing itself against a barrier, and that delegations wear their earpieces whenever someone they meet is hearing voices."

She took a breath.

"On the matter of navigation, I refer Fleet to Lieutenant Ashgrove's full report and to the conference call of stardate 1.514. For the general record: Ondrevel, and the dark sphere beside it, were found four hundred leagues from their charted position, where Lieutenant Ashgrove predicted they would be on the evidence of two old sightings. We do not know why the plane moved, or whether all planes do. We know where it was, and where it is, and that it got from one to the other at a steady pace in a straight line. I'd like that written somewhere every navigator in the Fleet will see it. I'd like Lieutenant Ashgrove commended. She'd like it not to be called Ashgrove's Law. I have no opinion."

"She'd *love* it to be called Ashgrove's Law," said Sel.

"I have no opinion," said Rhosyn again. "Last. Ship took his first injury in this engagement, and his first fight, and did not need to be told twice to do either well. When we left Rosste, I was told that an ensouled ship would be a helm and a hull and a voice. I've found it to be a crewmate. That's all. Calder out."

She leaned back in the chair.

"You can add your addendum now," she said. "I know you're going to."

"I wasn't going to," said the ship, with dignity.

"Sel."

"I was only going to say thank you," said Sel. A pause. "And that the dark sphere beside Ondrevel has a little light on it now. Did you see? Right on the side facing the owls. It wasn't there before." Another pause. "I'm sure it's nothing. But I'd like to know whose it is."

Rhosyn looked at the scryer. There, a dozen leagues off, against the smooth dark curve of the companion sphere: a single faint point of light, like a candle left in a window.

"So would I," she said. "Log that, too."

"Logged."

Something landed on the arm of the captain's chair with a soft, heavy thump. Barnacle sat down, folded his wings, and very carefully set down, on the captain's knee, a large gray rat with one white foot. It was quite dead.

"Judge," said the tressym, "I'd like it entered into the record that the Admiral has been relieved of command."

Rhosyn looked at the rat. The rat, with great dignity, did not look at anything.

"Sel," she said. "Is that going in my log?"

"Addendum," said the ship happily, "in the ship's voice: rat."

"Calder *out*," said the captain.

Paladin, Oath of the Watchers, about 9th level (Divine Smite, Lay on Hands, an aura of protection; the Oath's watchfulness against extraplanar things fits her mission and is why the black door frightened her rather than surprised her). Background: twenty years a circuit magistrate on the river roads of Rosste before joining the Fleet; she rarely draws her sword and prefers depositions to duels. Her alignment sense (smell) costs her effort and leaves memories she can't wash out; she has smelled very bad people and has never smelled anything like the door. Wants: to do things right, in order, on the evidence. Fears: becoming a judge who decides before she hears. ## 3. Story Beats by Plot

**A-plot: The voice beyond the stars.** A grieving star-reader is being groomed by a soulless thing outside his world to open a door for something far worse.

1. *IV, The Golden Door.* Tamsk. A year after Wennet's death, a "voice" claiming to be her has been promising to come home through a door. Sel's golden door appears; Tamsk thinks it is hers; the voice tells him to beware the strangers. The thing on the barrier turns toward the closed door.
2. *III, The Thing on the Glass.* Calder, Sorrow, Barnacle, Sel. The crew see a barn-sized seed-thing pressed to Ondrevel's barrier, "talking through a door." Barnacle notes it seems to have no smell.
3. *V, Questions.* Calder and officers. Sel reports it moved toward his open portal; they choose shifting over portaling so nothing can follow. Calder takes Hob for the ghosts-and-gods conversation.
4. *VI, Lammerlow.* Calder, Hob, Brakka, Isk, Tamsk. First contact; the Star-Warden asks if the voice is theirs. Calder reads Tamsk's ledger like a deposition: the voice only ever said what he already knew, and dodged the one question he couldn't answer. Earpieces warm when it speaks. Calder smells Tamsk: good, with a thread of something running out of him. The sealed letter is introduced.
5. *VIII, They're Hurting Her.* Tamsk, away team, Tobin, Sorrow. Tamsk screams that the ship is hurting his daughter at the exact moment Tobin reports driving off the attacker; the correlation proves the voice and the creature are one. Tamsk lashes out with pact fire and flees to his circle. Calder forbids force.
6. *IX, The Dark Door.* Calder, Tamsk, Hob, Brakka. A black parody of Sel's door forms; Calder smells overwhelming evil behind it and nothing at all at the end of Tamsk's thread. Using the ledger, she shows the voice only read him; she offers a fair test. The voice guesses the letter says "sorry, I love you"; the letter actually names the star Patience and tells him to stop checking her sums. Tamsk scuffs out the sigil himself. The door collapses, giving Calder a glimpse of stacked layers and a dragged iron hook.
7. *XI, Patience.* Tamsk, Calder, Hob, Isk. Grief and honesty: Hob admits nobody knows where the dead go, but she wasn't in that thing. Sel plays *Oribella's Theme* through Calder's earpiece. Isk asks for more talks.
8. *XIII, Captain's Log.* Calder records her observations, unexplained, for Fleet.

**B-plot: The plane that walked.** Ondrevel isn't at its charted coordinates, and a gnome's sums suggest the planes themselves move.

1. *I, Captain's Log.* Calder, Sel. They arrive at Fleet's coordinates; nothing is there.
2. *II, The Empty Sea.* Pemmy, Calder, Sorrow, Tobin, Barnacle. No debris, so no destroyed plane. Pemmy sets the founding organic's fix against an "unconfirmed" salvager's sighting five years later: same plane, different place. Calder: witnesses who disagree on where agree on what. Pemmy makes a prediction, not a guess. Calder reports to Fleet; Fleet is frightened.
3. *III.* The crew find Ondrevel within four leagues of the prediction, and its companion sphere moved alongside it. "A whole system walking."
4. *XII, Tavern Rules.* Pemmy argues all planes may walk; Fleet demands her numbers and secrecy.
5. *XIII.* Calder logs the finding and asks that it reach every navigator; the companion sphere now shows a light.

**C-plot: A bard learns to be a ship.** Sel, less than a year a ship, takes his first fear, first wound and first fight.

1. *I.* Sel's unrequested addenda and humor establish him.
2. *V.* He confesses he slammed the door in fear; the captain says he did right.
3. *VII, The Boarding.* Sel. The creature boards him and he feels pain for the first time. Sorrow tells him to drown the whispers; he plays *Orlalassar's Battle Theme*, and the crew rally. Barnacle cracks the seed-core and the creature flees.
4. *X, Nothing.* Sel follows it, waits for it to be distracted by Tamsk's refusal, and rams it. He asks if anyone was in it; Sorrow felt nothing. Tobin: "Well played, lad."
5. *XII.* The bawdy shanty in the tavern, with the captain's blessing.
6. *XIII.* Calder logs him as a crewmate, not a helm.

**D-runner: Barnacle and the Admiral.** A cat's war with a rat. II: the Admiral introduced. V: Barnacle stays aboard "to keep the rats in line." VII: he hunts the seed like a mouse. XIII: the Admiral is delivered to the captain's knee.

**How the plots intersect.** Pemmy's drift sums (B) are what get the ship to Ondrevel at all, and the same habit of trusting evidence over authority drives Calder's handling of Tamsk (A): two witnesses disagreeing on *where* in Act One become a voice that never answers *what* in Act Three. The creature's attack on Sel (C) is what produces the timing correlation that breaks the A-plot open, and Sel's patience in following the wounded creature lets him finish it at the moment Tamsk's refusal distracts it. Barnacle's nose (D) supplies the first evidence that the creature has no soul, which Calder's own sense confirms on the roof.
